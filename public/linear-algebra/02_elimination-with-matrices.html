Part 3 Explanation

The running example: the same portfolio, solved properly

In Lesson 1 we split ₹10 lakh between funds to hit a budget, a return and a market exposure. We found the answers by drawing pictures and by spotting tricks. That doesn't scale: with 50 funds and 50 rules nobody can draw or guess.

This lesson gives the method every computer uses: elimination. We apply it to the same two systems:

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 105 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{Nifty + bonds}} \qquad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 & 15 \\ 1 & 0 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 111 \\ 7.4 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{Nifty + bonds + midcap}} $$

Rows: budget (₹ lakh), return (₹ thousand), market exposure (₹ lakh). Unknowns: ₹ lakh in each fund.

1. The idea of elimination

Method · Gaussian elimination (forward elimination)

Subtract multiples of each pivot row to create zeros below the pivot, column by column, until the matrix is upper triangular. Named after Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777–1855), though the method is far older: it appears in China around 2,000 years ago, in The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art. It is the algorithm inside every equation solver.

Some systems are easy. If the last equation has only one unknown, you solve it at once, then work upwards:

$$ \begin{aligned} x + y + z &= 10 \\ -5y + 3z &= -9 \\ -0.4z &= -0.8 \end{aligned} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad z = 2,\; y = 3,\; x = 5. $$

Its matrix is upper triangular: zeros everywhere below the diagonal. Elimination turns any (good) system into one like this, without changing its solution.

Elimination in one sentence

Use the first equation to remove $x$ from every equation below it; then use the new second equation to remove $y$ from every equation below it; and so on, until the matrix is upper triangular. Strang: "it's the way every software package solves equations."

Two words do all the work:

  • The pivot is the diagonal entry we use to clear the entries below it.
  • The multiplier is how many times we subtract the pivot row: multiplier = (entry to clear) ÷ (pivot). The multiplier for row $i$, column $j$ is written $\ell_{ij}$ ("ell").
Why subtracting equations is allowed

If two statements are true, their difference is true too. Subtracting 12 × (budget rule) from the return rule gives a new rule that every correct portfolio still satisfies, and we can undo it by adding back (topic 8). So the solution never changes.

2. 2 × 2 by elimination

The two-fund portfolio: $x + y = 10$ (budget) and $12x + 7y = 105$ (return).

  1. Pivot: the $1$ in the top-left corner.
  2. Multiplier: the entry to clear is $12$, so $\ell_{21} = 12 / 1 = 12$.
  3. Subtract 12 × row 1 from row 2, on both sides: $(12x + 7y) - 12(x + y) = 105 - 12(10)$, which is $-5y = -15$.
$$ \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} \boxed{1} & 1 & 10 \\ 12 & 7 & 105 \end{array}\right] \;\xrightarrow{\;R_2 - 12R_1\;}\; \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} \boxed{1} & 1 & 10 \\ 0 & \boxed{-5} & -15 \end{array}\right] $$

Now back-substitute: $-5y = -15$ gives $y = 3$, then $x + 3 = 10$ gives $x = 7$. ₹7 lakh Nifty, ₹3 lakh bonds, the same answer as Lesson 1. The pivots are $1$ and $-5$.

Check yourself (MIT problem 2.1): eliminate $2x + 3y = 5$, $6x + 15y = 12$. What are the multiplier and the pivots?

$\ell_{21} = 6/2 = 3$. Row 2 − 3 × row 1 gives $6y = -3$. Pivots $2$ and $6$. Back-substitute: $y = -\tfrac12$, then $2x = 5 + \tfrac32$, so $x = \tfrac{13}{4}$.

3. 3 × 3, step by step

Method · Augmented matrix $[A \mid b]$

Write $b$ as an extra column next to $A$, so every row operation is applied to both sides of the equations at once.

Write the coefficients and the right-hand side side by side. Tacking $b$ on as an extra column gives the augmented matrix $[A \mid b]$, so whatever we do to an equation's left side we also do to its right side.

$$ [A \mid b] = \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 1 & 10 \\ 12 & 7 & 15 & 111 \\ 1 & 0 & 1.2 & 7.4 \end{array}\right] \quad \begin{matrix} \text{budget} \\ \text{return} \\ \text{exposure} \end{matrix} $$

Column 1: clear everything below the first pivot

The first pivot is the $1$ in the top-left corner.

  • Step (2, 1): $\ell_{21} = 12/1 = 12$. Row 2 − 12 × row 1: $(12, 7, 15, 111) - (12, 12, 12, 120) = (0, -5, 3, -9)$.
  • Step (3, 1): $\ell_{31} = 1/1 = 1$. Row 3 − 1 × row 1: $(1, 0, 1.2, 7.4) - (1, 1, 1, 10) = (0, -1, 0.2, -2.6)$.
$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{1} & 1 & 1 & 10 \\ 0 & -5 & 3 & -9 \\ 0 & -1 & 0.2 & -2.6 \end{array}\right] $$

The Nifty amount $x$ is now gone from rules 2 and 3. They form a smaller 2 × 2 system in $y$ and $z$, and we simply repeat.

Column 2: clear below the second pivot

The second pivot is the $-5$ that has appeared in row 2, column 2.

  • Step (3, 2): $\ell_{32} = (-1)/(-5) = 0.2$. Row 3 − 0.2 × row 2: $(0, -1, 0.2, -2.6) - (0, -1, 0.6, -1.8) = (0, 0, -0.4, -0.8)$.
$$ [U \mid c] = \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{1} & 1 & 1 & 10 \\ 0 & \boxed{-5} & 3 & -9 \\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{-0.4} & -0.8 \end{array}\right] $$
Result of elimination

$A$ has become the upper triangular matrix $U$, and $b$ has become $c = (10, -9, -0.8)$. The new system $Ux = c$ has exactly the same solution as $Ax = b$. The pivots $1, -5, -0.4$ sit on the diagonal of $U$.

A preview Strang drops in passing: multiply the pivots and you get the determinant of $A$. Here $1 \times (-5) \times (-0.4) = 2$. Determinants come much later.

4. Back substitution

Method · Back substitution

Solve an upper triangular system $Ux = c$ from the last equation up. Each equation brings in exactly one new unknown.

$Ux = c$ is triangular, so solve it from the bottom up. Each equation has only one new unknown:

  1. Row 3: $-0.4z = -0.8$, so $z = 2$.
  2. Row 2: $-5y + 3(2) = -9$, so $-5y = -15$ and $y = 3$.
  3. Row 1: $x + 3 + 2 = 10$, so $x = 5$.

₹5 lakh Nifty, ₹3 lakh bonds, ₹2 lakh midcap, the answer Lesson 1 found by substitution. Check it in the original rules: $5 + 3 + 2 = 10$ ✓, $60 + 21 + 30 = 111$ ✓, $5 + 0 + 2.4 = 7.4$ ✓.

The whole method

Forward elimination ($A \to U$, $b \to c$), then back substitution ($Ux = c$, bottom row first). That is all a computer does to solve $Ax = b$, just faster and with millions of rows.

Check yourself (Strang's lecture example): eliminate $x + 2y + z = 2$, $3x + 8y + z = 12$, $4y + z = 2$.

$\ell_{21} = 3$: row 2 becomes $2y - 2z = 6$. $\ell_{31} = 0$: nothing to do. $\ell_{32} = 4/2 = 2$: row 3 becomes $5z = -10$. Pivots $1, 2, 5$. Back substitution: $z = -2$, then $2y + 4 = 6$ so $y = 1$, then $x + 2 - 2 = 2$ so $x = 2$.

5. When elimination breaks down

Pivots can never be 0: we divide by them to find multipliers. So what if a 0 lands in a pivot position? Strang separates two cases.

Temporary failure: fixed by a row exchange

Method · Row exchange (pivoting)

When a pivot position holds 0, swap that row with a lower row that has a non-zero entry in the same column. Strang swaps only when forced. Software usually also swaps to bring the largest entry into the pivot position (partial pivoting), which keeps rounding errors small.

Replace the bond fund with a credit fund that also earns 12% but has no market exposure (beta 0). Its column is $(1, 12, 0)$. With target $(10, 126, 7.4)$:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 1 & 10 \\ 12 & 12 & 15 & 126 \\ 1 & 0 & 1.2 & 7.4 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{\;R_2 - 12R_1,\;\; R_3 - R_1\;} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{1} & 1 & 1 & 10 \\ 0 & \mathbf{0} & 3 & 6 \\ 0 & -1 & 0.2 & -2.6 \end{array}\right] $$

A $0$ in the second pivot position: Nifty and the credit fund earn the same, so removing $x$ from the return rule removed $y$ too. But there is a non-zero entry below it, so exchange rows 2 and 3:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{1} & 1 & 1 & 10 \\ 0 & \boxed{-1} & 0.2 & -2.6 \\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{3} & 6 \end{array}\right] $$

Now it is upper triangular, so back-substitute from the bottom row up:

  1. Row 3: $3z = 6$, so $z = 2$.
  2. Row 2: $-y + 0.2z = -2.6$. Put in $z = 2$: $-y + 0.4 = -2.6$, so $-y = -3$ and $y = 3$.
  3. Row 1: $x + y + z = 10$. Put in $y = 3$, $z = 2$: $x + 5 = 10$, so $x = 5$.

₹5 lakh Nifty, ₹3 lakh credit fund, ₹2 lakh midcap. Check in the original rules: budget $5 + 3 + 2 = 10$ ✓; return $12(5) + 12(3) + 15(2) = 60 + 36 + 30 = 126$ ✓; exposure $5 + 0 + 1.2(2) = 7.4$ ✓.

Three pivots ($1, -1, 3$) after the exchange, so the matrix is fine (invertible). Swapping the order of two equations never changes the solution.

Permanent failure: the matrix is singular

Now replace the midcap fund with the balanced fund from Lesson 1 (half Nifty, half bonds), column $(1, 9.5, 0.5)$:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 1 & 10 \\ 12 & 7 & 9.5 & 111 \\ 1 & 0 & 0.5 & 7.4 \end{array}\right] \longrightarrow \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{1} & 1 & 1 & 10 \\ 0 & \boxed{-5} & -2.5 & -9 \\ 0 & -1 & -0.5 & -2.6 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{\;R_3 - 0.2R_2\;} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{1} & 1 & 1 & 10 \\ 0 & \boxed{-5} & -2.5 & -9 \\ 0 & 0 & \mathbf{0} & -0.8 \end{array}\right] $$

A $0$ in the third pivot position and nothing below it to swap in. Only two pivots. The last row reads $0 = -0.8$: no solution. (With a target on the funds' plane, such as $(10, 95, 5)$, the last row becomes $0 = 0$ and there are infinitely many solutions.)

Pivots decide everything

An $n \times n$ matrix is invertible exactly when elimination (with row exchanges where needed) produces $n$ non-zero pivots. Fewer pivots means singular: no solution or infinitely many, just as the pictures in Lesson 1 showed.

The 2 × 2 version: two Nifty funds give $\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 12 \end{smallmatrix}\right] \to \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$, one pivot, and the return rule becomes $0 = -15$.

Check yourself: in Strang's example, which number in place of the 8 causes a temporary failure, and which number in place of the last 1 causes a permanent one?

An $8 \to 6$ makes the second pivot $0$, but row 3 has a $4$ below it, so a row exchange fixes it. A last entry of $-4$ instead of $1$ makes the third pivot $-4 - (-4) = 0$ with nothing below: permanent failure, singular matrix.

6. Elimination as matrices

Method · Elimination matrices $E_{ij}$

Write each row operation as a left multiplication by an elementary matrix: the identity with $-\ell_{ij}$ in position $(i, j)$. All of elimination becomes $E_{32}E_{31}E_{21}A = U$.

In topic 3 we did three row operations by hand. Strang's main point is that each of them is a multiplication by a matrix. Once that is true, the whole of elimination fits in one line, and that line is what the next lessons build on.

How a matrix can change rows

Lesson 1's key fact was about columns: matrix × column mixes the columns (Lesson 1). Its twin is about rows:

Row × matrix mixes the rows

$\begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \end{bmatrix} A = a\,(\text{row } 1 \text{ of } A) + b\,(\text{row } 2) + c\,(\text{row } 3)$.

Try it on our portfolio matrix with the row $\begin{bmatrix} -12 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} -12 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 & 15 \\ 1 & 0 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix} = -12\,(1, 1, 1) + 1\,(12, 7, 15) = (0, -5, 3). $$

That is exactly the new return rule from step (2, 1): "return − 12 × budget". So a matrix whose rows are recipes like this can rebuild every row at once:

Row of the matrixRecipe it followsNew row
$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$keep row 1budget, unchanged
$\begin{bmatrix} -12 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$row 2 − 12 × row 1return with $x$ removed
$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$keep row 3exposure, unchanged

Stacking those three rows gives the elimination matrix $E_{21}$. It is the identity matrix $I$ (which keeps every row) with one extra entry, $-12$, in row 2, column 1.

Elimination matrix $E_{ij}$

The identity matrix with $-\ell_{ij}$ in row $i$, column $j$. Multiplying by it subtracts $\ell_{ij}$ × row $j$ from row $i$ and leaves every other row alone.

The whole of topic 3, as three multiplications

Step (2, 1), $\ell_{21} = 12$: subtract 12 × budget from return.

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -12 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{E_{21}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 & 15 \\ 1 & 0 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 & 3 \\ 1 & 0 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step (3, 1), $\ell_{31} = 1$: subtract budget from exposure.

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{E_{31}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 & 3 \\ 1 & 0 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 & 3 \\ 0 & -1 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step (3, 2), $\ell_{32} = 0.2$: subtract 0.2 × the new row 2 from row 3.

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -0.2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{E_{32}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 & 3 \\ 0 & -1 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & -0.4 \end{bmatrix} = U $$

The same three matrices turn $b = (10, 111, 7.4)$ into $(10, -9, 7.4)$, then $(10, -9, -2.6)$, then $c = (10, -9, -0.8)$. Written as one line:

$$ E_{32}\,E_{31}\,E_{21}\,A = U \qquad\text{and}\qquad E_{32}\,E_{31}\,E_{21}\,b = c. $$

One matrix for all of elimination

Matrix multiplication is associative: you can move the parentheses, $E_{32}(E_{31}(E_{21}A)) = (E_{32}E_{31}E_{21})A$. So multiply the three $E$'s first and you get a single matrix that does every step at once:

$$ E = E_{32}E_{31}E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -12 & 1 & 0 \\ 1.4 & -0.2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad EA = U. $$

Check its bottom row with the row rule: $1.4(1, 1, 1) - 0.2(12, 7, 15) + 1(1, 0, 1.2) = (1.4 - 2.4 + 1,\ 1.4 - 1.4 + 0,\ 1.4 - 3 + 1.2) = (0, 0, -0.4)$ ✓, the last row of $U$.

Watch out: order matters

You may move parentheses, but you may not reorder: in general $AB \ne BA$. The first step sits next to $A$, the last step on the far left. Swap two of them and you get a different matrix:

$$ E_{32}E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -12 & 1 & 0 \\ 2.4 & -0.2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\text{but}\qquad E_{21}E_{32} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -12 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -0.2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Why: $E_{21}E_{32}$ does $E_{32}$ first, so it subtracts 0.2 × the old return row $(12, 7, 15)$ instead of the new one $(0, -5, 3)$. Same operations, different order, different result.

Notice the messy $1.4$ in $E$, even though the multipliers were the clean numbers $12, 1, 0.2$. Going the other way, from $U$ back to $A$, keeps them clean: that is Lesson 4, $A = LU$.

Check yourself: what does the row $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$ times a 3 × 3 matrix produce? (Strang's example)

A row: $1 \times$ row 1 $+ 2 \times$ row 2 $+ 7 \times$ row 3 of that matrix.

7. Row exchanges: permutation matrices

Method · Permutation matrix $P$

The identity with its rows reordered. $PA$ reorders the rows of $A$; $AP$ reorders its columns.

The row exchange in topic 5 (the credit fund) is also a matrix. After column 1 we had a $0$ in the second pivot position:

$$ M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & \mathbf{0} & 3 \\ 0 & -1 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix} $$

To build the matrix that swaps rows 2 and 3, do that swap to the identity matrix:

$$ P_{23} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad P_{23}M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 0.2 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Read its rows as recipes: "take row 1", "take row 3", "take row 2". A matrix that only reorders rows is a permutation matrix. For the credit-fund system the whole elimination is $P_{23}\,E_{31}\,E_{21}\,A = U$.

Left acts on rows, right acts on columns

Take the two-fund matrix $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and the swap $P = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$:

$$ PA = \begin{bmatrix} 12 & 7 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \;\;\text{(rows swapped)} \qquad\qquad AP = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 7 & 12 \end{bmatrix} \;\;\text{(columns swapped)} $$
The rule

A matrix on the left acts on rows: all of elimination works this way. A matrix on the right acts on columns. $PA \ne AP$: one more case of $AB \ne BA$.

In portfolio terms: $PA$ lists the rules in a different order (return first, then budget), which never changes the answer. $AP$ swaps the funds, so the first unknown becomes the bond amount: the answer comes out as $(3, 7)$ instead of $(7, 3)$.

8. Undoing a step: inverses

Method · Inverse of an elimination step

Flip the sign of the multiplier: $E_{ij}^{-1}$ adds back what $E_{ij}$ subtracted. For a row exchange, swap again. Inverses of whole matrices use the Gauss–Jordan method, covered in Lesson 3.

$E_{21}$ subtracted 12 × budget from return. To undo it, add 12 × budget back. That is the identity with $+12$ where $E_{21}$ had $-12$:

$$ E_{21}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 12 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Check it on the portfolio: $E_{21}$ turned the return row $(12, 7, 15)$ into $(0, -5, 3)$. Adding 12 × budget gives $(0, -5, 3) + 12(1, 1, 1) = (12, 7, 15)$: the original return rule is back. So $E_{21}^{-1}(E_{21}A) = A$, and $E_{21}^{-1}E_{21} = I$.

Inverse

The matrix that undoes $E$ is its inverse, written $E^{-1}$, with $E^{-1}E = I$. For an elimination matrix, just flip the sign of the multiplier. For a row exchange, swap again: $P_{23}^{-1} = P_{23}$.

Why elimination gives the right answer

Every step can be undone, so no information is lost or added. Any portfolio that satisfies the original rules $Ax = b$ also satisfies the new rules $Ux = c$, and the other way round. That's why the answer we read off $U$, ₹5 lakh, ₹3 lakh and ₹2 lakh, is the answer to the original question.

Inverses of whole matrices (not just single steps), and when they exist, are the next lesson.

Check yourself: what is the inverse of $E_{32}$, which subtracts 0.2 × row 2 from row 3?

Add 0.2 × row 2 back to row 3: the identity with $+0.2$ in row 3, column 2.

9. Summary

Key ideas

  1. Elimination turns $Ax = b$ into an upper triangular $Ux = c$ with the same solution: clear each column below its pivot, using multiplier $\ell_{ij} = $ entry ÷ pivot. (topic 1)
  2. Do every step to the augmented matrix $[A \mid b]$, so the right-hand side comes along. (topic 3)
  3. Back substitution solves $Ux = c$ from the last row up. The portfolio: $z = 2$, $y = 3$, $x = 5$. (topic 4)
  4. A 0 in a pivot position: exchange with a lower row if you can (temporary failure); if nothing below is non-zero, the matrix is singular. $n$ pivots ⇔ invertible. (topic 5)
  5. Each step is a multiplication by an elimination matrix: $E_{32}E_{31}E_{21}A = U$. Row × matrix combines rows. (topic 6)
  6. You can move parentheses (associative) but not swap order: $AB \ne BA$. Left multiplication acts on rows, right on columns. (topic 6, topic 7)
  7. Every step is reversible ($E^{-1}$ adds back what $E$ subtracted), so elimination never changes the solution. (topic 8)

Example 1: Hedging by elimination

Recall the setup: sell $x$ Nifty futures, $y$ Nifty IT futures and $z$ Bank Nifty futures to cancel market, currency and rate exposure. Each column is a hedge's risk profile.

2 × 2: already triangular

$$ \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 1 & 1 & 8 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \end{array}\right] \quad \begin{matrix} \text{market} \\ \text{currency} \end{matrix} $$

There is already a $0$ below the first pivot (Nifty futures carry no currency risk), so $\ell_{21} = 0$ and there is nothing to eliminate. Pivots $1, 1$. Back substitution: $y = 3$, then $x = 8 - 3 = 5$. Sell 5 Nifty and 3 Nifty IT futures.

The same problem, rules written in a different order

Suppose you list the currency rule first. Nothing about the hedge has changed, but now:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 & 8 \end{array}\right] \quad \begin{matrix} \text{currency} \\ \text{market} \end{matrix} $$

A $0$ in the first pivot position. There is a non-zero entry below it, so this is a temporary failure: exchange the rows with $P = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and you are back to the triangular system above. The same happens in 3 × 3 with the rules ordered (currency, market, rates):

$$ P_{12}\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 0 & 1 & 0 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & 10 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{1} & 1 & 1 & 10 \\ 0 & \boxed{1} & 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{1} & 2 \end{array}\right] \quad\Rightarrow\quad z = 2,\; y = 3,\; x = 10 - 3 - 2 = 5. $$

Sell 5 Nifty, 3 Nifty IT and 2 Bank Nifty futures. Hedging problems often arrive nearly triangular: each special hedge (IT for currency, Bank Nifty for rates) touches only its own risk plus the market.

Singular: no pivot for currency

With a Nifty ETF in place of Nifty IT futures, both columns are $(1, 0)$:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} \boxed{1} & 1 & 8 \\ 0 & \mathbf{0} & 3 \end{array}\right] $$

A $0$ in the second pivot position and no row below to swap in: one pivot only, so the matrix is singular. The last row says $0 = 3$: no hedge exists, exactly as Lesson 1's picture showed.

Check yourself: with rows ordered (rates, currency, market), which permutation makes the 3 × 3 hedging matrix triangular?

The rows are $(0, 0, 1)$, $(0, 1, 0)$, $(1, 1, 1)$. Exchanging rows 1 and 3 ($P_{13}$) puts $(1, 1, 1)$ on top and gives the triangular matrix again.

Example 2: Lot sizes by elimination

Recall the setup: $x, y, z$ are lots of each product, rows are expiry scenarios, entries are payoffs in Nifty points per lot.

2 × 2: futures and the 20,000 call

$$ \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} -200 & 0 & -200 \\ 200 & 200 & 600 \end{array}\right] \quad \begin{matrix} \text{Nifty at 19,800} \\ \text{Nifty at 20,200} \end{matrix} $$

Pivot $-200$. Multiplier $\ell_{21} = 200 / (-200) = -1$. Subtracting $-1$ times row 1 means adding row 1:

$$ \xrightarrow{\;R_2 - (-1)R_1\;} \left[\begin{array}{cc|c} \boxed{-200} & 0 & -200 \\ 0 & \boxed{200} & 400 \end{array}\right] $$

Back substitution: row 2 gives $200y = 400$, so $y = 2$. Row 1 gives $-200x + 0(2) = -200$, so $x = 1$.

1 lot of futures, 2 lots of the call. A negative multiplier is fine; only a zero pivot is a problem.

3 × 3: futures, 19,900 call, 20,100 call

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} -200 & 0 & 0 & -200 \\ 0 & 100 & 0 & 100 \\ 200 & 300 & 100 & 700 \end{array}\right] \quad \begin{matrix} 19{,}800 \\ 20{,}000 \\ 20{,}200 \end{matrix} $$
  • Step (2, 1): already $0$, so $\ell_{21} = 0$.
  • Step (3, 1): $\ell_{31} = 200/(-200) = -1$, so add row 1 to row 3: $(0, 300, 100, 500)$.
  • Step (3, 2): $\ell_{32} = 300/100 = 3$, so row 3 − 3 × row 2: $(0, 0, 100, 200)$.
$$ [U \mid c] = \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{-200} & 0 & 0 & -200 \\ 0 & \boxed{100} & 0 & 100 \\ 0 & 0 & \boxed{100} & 200 \end{array}\right] $$

Back substitution, bottom row first: $100z = 200$ gives $z = 2$; $100y + 0(2) = 100$ gives $y = 1$; $-200x + 0 + 0 = -200$ gives $x = 1$. Check at 20,200: $200(1) + 300(1) + 100(2) = 700$ ✓.

1 lot of futures, 1 lot of the 19,900 call, 2 lots of the 20,100 call. As matrices: $E_{32}E_{31}A = U$ with

$$ E_{31} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad E_{32} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

$E_{31}$ has $+1$, not $-1$: the entry is always $-\ell_{ij}$, and here $\ell_{31} = -1$.

Singular: futures, call and put at one strike

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} -200 & 0 & 200 & -200 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 100 \\ 200 & 200 & 0 & 700 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{\;R_3 + R_1\;} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{-200} & 0 & 200 & -200 \\ 0 & \mathbf{0} & 0 & 100 \\ 0 & 200 & 200 & 500 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{\;P_{23}\;} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \boxed{-200} & 0 & 200 & -200 \\ 0 & \boxed{200} & 200 & 500 \\ 0 & 0 & \mathbf{0} & 100 \end{array}\right] $$

The second pivot position held a $0$, and a row exchange fixed it. But then the third pivot position holds a $0$ with nothing below: two pivots, singular. The last row says $0 = 100$: these three products can't pay 100 points at 20,000, because none of them pays anything there. Put–call parity (futures = call − put) is the hidden dependence.

Check yourself: in the 3 × 3 lot system, what are the pivots, and what is their product?

Pivots $-200, 100, 100$. Product $-2{,}000{,}000$: non-zero, so the matrix is invertible (and that product is its determinant).
