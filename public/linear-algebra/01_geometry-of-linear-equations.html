Part 3 Explanation

The running example: a portfolio mix

You have ₹10 lakh to invest and two funds to choose from:

FundExpected return per yearReturn on ₹1 lakh
Nifty 50 index fund12%₹12 thousand
Government bond fund7%₹7 thousand

You want to invest all ₹10 lakh and earn ₹1,05,000 a year (₹105 thousand, a 10.5% return). How much goes into each fund?

The returns are illustrative round numbers, not forecasts.

1. The problem: $Ax = b$

Give the two amounts letters. These are the unknowns:

  • $x$ = ₹ lakh put in the Nifty fund,
  • $y$ = ₹ lakh put in the bond fund.

Now write each requirement as an equation:

  • Budget rule. The two amounts must add up to ₹10 lakh: $\;x + y = 10$.
  • Return rule. Each lakh in Nifty earns ₹12 thousand and each lakh in bonds earns ₹7 thousand, and together they must earn ₹105 thousand: $\;12x + 7y = 105$.
$$ \begin{aligned} x + y &= 10 &&\text{(budget, ₹ lakh)}\\ 12x + 7y &= 105 &&\text{(return, ₹ thousand)} \end{aligned} $$

Two equations that must both be true at once are a system of equations. A solution is a pair $(x, y)$ that passes both rules.

The numbers that multiply the unknowns (the $1, 1$ and $12, 7$) are coefficients.

Definition: linear equation

An equation is linear when each unknown is only multiplied by a number and the pieces are added. No squares, no unknowns multiplied together, no $\sqrt{x}$ or $\sin x$.

Linear ✓Not linear ✗
$x + y = 10$$x^2 + y = 10$ (a square)
$12x + 7y = 105$$xy = 21$ (unknowns multiplied together)

"Linear" has the word line in it: a linear equation in two unknowns draws a straight line.

The fundamental problem of linear algebra is to solve such a system: $n$ linear equations in $n$ unknowns. This lesson looks at one system in three ways.

Only the right-hand side of the return rule changes: $x + y = 10$ and $12x + 7y = 100$.

2. Vectors and combinations

Look at what ₹1 lakh in the Nifty fund does to our two rules. It uses ₹1 lakh of the budget and earns ₹12 thousand of return. Write those two numbers as a column:

$$ \text{Nifty fund: } \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 12 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{Bond fund: } \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \leftarrow \begin{matrix} \text{budget used (₹ lakh)} \\ \text{return earned (₹ thousand)} \end{matrix}$$
Definition: vector

A vector is an ordered list of numbers, written as a column. Each number is a component. The Nifty vector $(1, 12)$ has components $1$ and $12$, and the order matters: first budget, then return.

A vector with 2 components can be drawn as an arrow from the origin: here, 1 step along the budget axis and 12 steps up the return axis.

The set of all vectors with 2 components is called $\mathbb{R}^2$ (say "R-two"). $\mathbb{R}$ means the real numbers, and the little 2 says how many components. Likewise $\mathbb{R}^3$ is all lists of 3 numbers (3D space) and $\mathbb{R}^n$ is all lists of $n$ numbers, which we can't draw once $n > 3$ but can still calculate with.

Two moves: scale and add

  • Scalar multiplication. Multiply every component by one number (a scalar). ₹7 lakh in Nifty is 7 copies of ₹1 lakh: $\;7\begin{bmatrix}1\\12\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}7\\84\end{bmatrix}$. Same direction, 7 times as long.
  • Vector addition. Add matching components. ₹1 lakh in each fund: $\;\begin{bmatrix}1\\12\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}1\\7\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\19\end{bmatrix}$: ₹2 lakh used, ₹19 thousand earned. As arrows: walk the first arrow, then walk the second from where you stopped.
Definition: linear combination

Scale some vectors, then add them. For vectors $c, d$ and numbers $x, y$, the vector $\;x\,c + y\,d\;$ is a linear combination of $c$ and $d$. The numbers $x, y$ are its weights.

In our example a linear combination of the two fund vectors is simply a portfolio: $x$ lakh of Nifty plus $y$ lakh of bonds. Strang calls this "the most fundamental operation in the whole course".

Check yourself: what vector describes ₹4 lakh in Nifty plus ₹2 lakh in bonds?

$4\begin{bmatrix}1\\12\end{bmatrix} + 2\begin{bmatrix}1\\7\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4\\48\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}2\\14\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}6\\62\end{bmatrix}$: ₹6 lakh used, ₹62 thousand earned.

3. Matrix form

Put all the coefficients in one table. A matrix is just a rectangular table of numbers:

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}}_{x} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 105 \end{bmatrix}}_{b} \qquad\text{or simply}\qquad Ax = b. $$
SymbolNameIn our example
$A$coefficient matrixrow $i$ = rule $i$ (budget, return); column $j$ = fund $j$ (Nifty, bond)
$x$vector of unknownsthe amounts $(x, y)$ in ₹ lakh
$b$right-hand sidethe targets $(10, 105)$

A matrix with $m$ rows and $n$ columns is called $m \times n$ ("$m$ by $n$"). Ours is $2 \times 2$: 2 rules, 2 funds. Notice the columns of $A$ are exactly the fund vectors from topic 2.

Watch out

The letter $x$ does two jobs: the single unknown $x$ (Nifty amount) and the whole vector of unknowns $x = (x, y)$. Strang writes the vector in bold. From the context it is always clear which one is meant.

What $Ax$ means: matrix × vector, two ways

What does $Ax$ actually mean? Take our 2 × 2 matrix and the portfolio $x = (7, 3)$. There are two ways to multiply, and both give the same answer.

Way 1: by columns (Strang's favourite).

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = 7\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 12 \end{bmatrix} + 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 + 3 \\ 84 + 21 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 105 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Read it as "7 lakh of fund 1 plus 3 lakh of fund 2". This is the idea behind the column picture (topic 5).

Way 2: by rows (dot products). Multiply each row of $A$ by $x$, entry by entry, and add. This is a dot product:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\cdot 7 + 1\cdot 3 \\ 12\cdot 7 + 7\cdot 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 105 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Each row answers one rule: row 1 · $x$ = budget used, row 2 · $x$ = return earned. This is the idea behind the row picture (topic 4).

Key fact

$Ax$ is a combination of the columns of $A$, with the entries of $x$ as the weights. So "$Ax = b$ has a solution" means exactly "$b$ is a combination of the columns".

Size rule

An $m \times n$ matrix times a vector with $n$ entries gives a vector with $m$ entries. The number of columns of $A$ must equal the number of entries of $x$: one weight per column (one amount per fund).

More generally $(m \times n)\,(n \times p) = (m \times p)$: the inner sizes must match, the outer sizes give the answer's shape.

Check yourself: compute $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ both ways (Strang's example).

By columns: $1(2, 1) + 2(5, 3) = (2 + 10,\ 1 + 6) = (12, 7)$. By rows: $2\cdot1 + 5\cdot2 = 12$ and $1\cdot1 + 3\cdot2 = 7$. Same answer, $(12, 7)$.

Check yourself: true or false? A 3 × 2 matrix times a 2 × 3 matrix is a 3 × 3 matrix.

True. The inner sizes (2 and 2) match, and the outer sizes give $3 \times 3$.

4. The row picture

Idea: take one equation (one row) at a time and draw every point $(x, y)$ that satisfies it. A linear equation in two unknowns gives a straight line. A solution must satisfy both rules, so it sits on both lines: it is where the lines cross.

Draw each line from two points

Two points fix a line. An easy method: set one unknown to $0$ and solve for the other.

Budget: $x + y = 10$Return: $12x + 7y = 105$
Put $x = 0$$y = 10$ → point $(0, 10)$$7y = 105$, $y = 15$ → point $(0, 15)$
Put $y = 0$$x = 10$ → point $(10, 0)$$12x = 105$, $x = 8.75$ → point $(8.75, 0)$
Meaningall-bonds or all-Nifty, both use ₹10 lakh₹15 lakh in bonds, or ₹8.75 lakh in Nifty, each earns ₹105 thousand

Check the crossing point in both rules. Reading a point off a picture is not proof; the arithmetic is.

  • Budget: $7 + 3 = 10$ ✓
  • Return: $12 \times 7 + 7 \times 3 = 84 + 21 = 105$ ✓

So the answer is ₹7 lakh in the Nifty fund and ₹3 lakh in the bond fund.

Tip: does the line pass through the origin?

Strang always asks this first. A line passes through $(0, 0)$ exactly when its right-hand side is $0$. Neither of our lines does: investing nothing can't use ₹10 lakh or earn ₹105 thousand.

The row picture is the one you met in school, and it works well for two unknowns. With three unknowns each rule becomes a plane, and it gets hard to see (topic 7).

Check yourself: where does the budget line cross the return line if the target return is ₹1,20,000?

Solve $x + y = 10$ and $12x + 7y = 120$. From the first, $y = 10 - x$. Then $12x + 70 - 7x = 120$, so $5x = 50$, $x = 10$, $y = 0$: all ₹10 lakh in Nifty. The lines cross on the $x$-axis at $(10, 0)$.

5. The column picture ★

Now read the same system a column at a time. Pull out everything that multiplies $x$ and everything that multiplies $y$:

$$ x \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 12 \end{bmatrix} + y \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 105 \end{bmatrix} $$

Read it aloud: "$x$ lakh of Nifty plus $y$ lakh of bonds must give ₹10 lakh used and ₹105 thousand earned." The left side is a linear combination of the columns of $A$. So the question has become:

The column question

Which combination of the column vectors $(1, 12)$ and $(1, 7)$ produces the target vector $b = (10, 105)$?

We already know the answer from the row picture, $x = 7$ and $y = 3$, so draw that combination: 7 copies of the Nifty arrow, then 3 copies of the bond arrow from its tip.

Component by component: budget $7 + 3 = 10$, return $84 + 21 = 105$. Same answer as the row picture, seen a completely different way.

Try it: mix the two funds

Move the sliders. The blue arrow is your Nifty amount, the green arrow your bond amount, and the dot is where your portfolio lands. Can you hit the target ring?

Check yourself: in the column picture, what does the point $(10, 70)$ mean, and which portfolio reaches it?

₹10 lakh used and ₹70 thousand earned, a 7% return. Only bonds earn 7%, so $x = 0$, $y = 10$: $0\cdot(1, 12) + 10\cdot(1, 7) = (10, 70)$.

6. Which targets can we reach?

Strang now asks a bigger question: if we try every $x$ and every $y$, which right-hand sides $b$ can we produce? In money terms: which (budget, return) targets can some mix of these two funds hit?

Result

The combinations of $(1, 12)$ and $(1, 7)$ fill the whole plane $\mathbb{R}^2$. For every target $b$ there is exactly one mix $(x, y)$.

Why: the two arrows point in different directions. One arrow alone only reaches points on its own line (see the scaling figure in topic 2). A second arrow pointing a different way lets you move off that line, and with both you can reach anywhere.

Why it works: solve for any target $(B, R)$

Take any budget $B$ and any return $R$. We need $x + y = B$ and $12x + 7y = R$.

From the first, $y = B - x$. Put that into the second: $12x + 7(B - x) = R$, so $5x + 7B = R$, giving

$$ x = \frac{R - 7B}{5}, \qquad y = B - x = \frac{12B - R}{5}. $$

We only divided by $5 = 12 - 7$, which is not zero because the two funds have different returns. So a solution exists for every $(B, R)$, and it is the only one. If the returns were equal we would be dividing by zero: that is the singular case in topic 8.

Definition: span

The span of some vectors is the set of every point you can reach by mixing them: scale each one by any number (positive, negative or zero) and add. In portfolio words: every (budget, return) result you can get from these funds, in any amounts.

  • Vectors = your available directions (here, the funds).
  • Span = everywhere you can reach using those directions.

It is not about one target. It is about the whole reachable area: a line, a plane, or a whole space.

See how the span grows as we add funds:

Funds you may useTheir spanWhat it means
Nifty only, $(1, 12)$a line through the origin: return $= 12 \times$ budgetwhatever you invest, you earn exactly 12%
Bonds only, $(1, 7)$a different line: return $= 7 \times$ budgetyou earn exactly 7%
Nifty and bondsthe whole plane $\mathbb{R}^2$any budget with any return (using negative amounts if needed)
Definition: column space

The column space of a matrix $A$ is the span of its columns. Strang writes it $C(A)$. For us the columns are the funds, so $C(A)$ is every target your funds can produce.

For our matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$, the column space is all of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

Why the column space matters

$Ax = b$ has a solution exactly when $b$ is in the column space of $A$.

The reason is one sentence: $Ax$ is always a combination of the columns of $A$ (see topic 3), so asking "is there an $x$ with $Ax = b$?" is the same as asking "is $b$ one of those combinations?".

  • Nifty + bonds: $C(A)$ is the whole plane, so every target, including $(10, 105)$, has a solution.
  • Two Nifty funds (topic 8): $C(A)$ is only the line return $= 12 \times$ budget. The target $(10, 120)$ is on it (solvable); $(10, 105)$ is not (no solution).
Watch out: maths says yes, the market may say no

"Every target" includes strange ones. Want a 15% return on ₹10 lakh, ₹150 thousand? The formula gives $x = (150 - 70)/5 = 16$ and $y = (120 - 150)/5 = -6$. A negative amount means borrowing ₹6 lakh at the bond rate to put ₹16 lakh into Nifty. The algebra is happy; whether you are allowed to (or should) do that is a separate question.

Check yourself: which mix earns exactly 10% (₹100 thousand) on ₹10 lakh?

$x = (100 - 70)/5 = 6$ and $y = (120 - 100)/5 = 4$. Check: $6 + 4 = 10$ ✓ and $72 + 28 = 100$ ✓.

7. Three funds, three rules: 3 × 3

Add a third fund and a third rule.

  • Midcap fund: expected 15% a year (₹15 thousand per lakh), but riskier.
  • Market exposure rule. A fund's beta says how much it moves when Nifty moves 1%. Nifty fund: beta $1.0$. Bond fund: beta $0$. Midcap fund: beta $1.2$. Each lakh of a fund adds "beta × 1 lakh" of Nifty-like exposure, and you want ₹7.4 lakh of exposure in total.

With $z$ = ₹ lakh in the midcap fund:

$$ \begin{aligned} x + y + z &= 10 &&\text{(1) budget}\\ 12x + 7y + 15z &= 111 &&\text{(2) return}\\ 1.0x \phantom{{}+7y} + 1.2z &= 7.4 &&\text{(3) market exposure} \end{aligned} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 & 15 \\ 1 & 0 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 111 \\ 7.4 \end{bmatrix}}_{b} $$

Now $A$ is $3 \times 3$. Each column is still one fund: (budget used, return earned, exposure added) per ₹1 lakh. The bond fund's column has a $0$ at the bottom because it adds no market exposure.

Solve it step by step

A systematic method (elimination) is the next lesson. Here, substitution is enough: each step removes one unknown.

  1. Equation (3) has no $y$. Write $x$ in terms of $z$: $\;x = 7.4 - 1.2z$.
  2. Equation (1) gives $y$. $y = 10 - x - z = 10 - (7.4 - 1.2z) - z = 2.6 + 0.2z$.
  3. Put both into equation (2). $12(7.4 - 1.2z) + 7(2.6 + 0.2z) + 15z = 111$. Multiply out: $88.8 - 14.4z + 18.2 + 1.4z + 15z = 111$, so $107 + 2z = 111$ and $z = 2$.
  4. Back-substitute. $x = 7.4 - 2.4 = 5$ and $y = 2.6 + 0.4 = 3$.

Check: budget $5 + 3 + 2 = 10$ ✓; return $60 + 21 + 30 = 111$ ✓; exposure $5 + 0 + 2.4 = 7.4$ ✓. So ₹5 lakh Nifty, ₹3 lakh bonds, ₹2 lakh midcap.

Row picture: three planes

With three unknowns, all the points $(x, y, z)$ satisfying one linear equation form a flat plane. Two planes usually meet in a line; the third plane cuts that line at one point, the solution.

Column picture: three arrows in 3D

$$ x\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 12 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + y\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 7 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + z\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 15 \\ 1.2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 111 \\ 7.4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Same question as before, one dimension up: which mix of the three fund arrows lands on $b$? Walk 5 Nifty arrows, then 3 bond arrows, then 2 midcap arrows.

Change only the target

Keep the same three funds (same $A$) but change the goal to ₹98 thousand return and ₹5.2 lakh exposure: $b = (10, 98, 5.2)$. The answer is $(4, 5, 1)$.

  • Check: $4 + 5 + 1 = 10$ ✓; $48 + 35 + 15 = 98$ ✓; $4 + 0 + 1.2 = 5.2$ ✓.
  • Row picture: three new planes (each right-hand side moved), now meeting at $(4, 5, 1)$.
  • Column picture: the same three arrows, just mixed in different amounts. This is why Strang prefers columns: the arrows belong to $A$ and never change; only the target does.
Check yourself: which mix of the three funds gives $b = (1, 15, 1.2)$?

$b$ is exactly the midcap column, so $x = 0$, $y = 0$, $z = 1$: ₹1 lakh in midcap. Strang uses the same trick in the lecture (his right-hand side equals column 3).

8. When it goes wrong: singular matrices

So far every target could be reached. Strang's key question:

The question, in two languages

Algebra: can we solve $Ax = b$ for every right-hand side $b$?

Columns: do the linear combinations of the columns fill the whole space?

These are the same question.

2 × 2: two funds that are really one

Replace the bond fund with a second Nifty 50 index fund from another company. It also earns 12%, so its column is also $(1, 12)$.

$$ x + y = 10, \qquad 12x + 12y = 105. $$

With these two funds you earn 12% whatever you do. If the target is ₹105 thousand there is no solution. If the target were exactly ₹120 thousand, every split works: the two lines would be the same line, giving infinitely many solutions.

Definition: singular and invertible

A square matrix is singular when its columns fill less than the whole space, so some $b$ cannot be reached. Its columns are dependent: one column adds nothing new.

Otherwise it is non-singular, also called invertible: the columns fill the whole space and every $b$ has exactly one solution. Our Nifty + bond matrix is invertible; the two-Nifty matrix is singular.

3 × 3: a fund that is just a mix of the others

Replace the midcap fund with a balanced fund that simply holds half Nifty, half bonds. Per lakh it uses ₹1 lakh, earns $\tfrac12(12) + \tfrac12(7) = 9.5$ thousand and adds $\tfrac12(1) + \tfrac12(0) = 0.5$ lakh of exposure:

$$ \text{balanced} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 9.5 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix} = \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 12 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 7 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. $$

The third column is a combination of the first two, so all three columns lie in one plane. Every portfolio you can build lies in that plane too, and any target off it is unreachable. This is exactly Strang's warning: "if these three columns all lie in the same plane, then their combinations will lie in that same plane".

Watch out

Three funds do not guarantee three independent directions. A fund built from the others adds no new direction, however different its name or factsheet looks.

With many funds

Nothing changes with 9 funds and 9 rules: each column is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^9$ and the question is still "which mix of the columns reaches $b$?". You can't draw it, but the column picture still works. Funds picked at random almost always fill the whole space (invertible). Two copies of the same Nifty 50 index from different companies don't: the columns then fill only an 8-dimensional "plane" inside 9-dimensional space, and most targets are out of reach.

Check yourself: is the target $(10, 111, 7.4)$ reachable with Nifty, bond and balanced funds?

Every reachable target $(B, R, E)$ satisfies $-7B + R - 5E = 0$ (the equation of the plane; each column passes, e.g. Nifty: $-7 + 12 - 5 = 0$). For $(10, 111, 7.4)$: $-70 + 111 - 37 = 4 \ne 0$. So no: it is off the plane.

1. The agenda: what question are we solving?

The question

A client says: "I want a position that pays me these amounts depending on where Nifty expires." You can only trade what is on the screen: a few CEs, PEs, a future, a bond.

How many lots of each product give exactly the client's payoff at every Nifty level?

This lecture's angle: see the answer. The same question can be drawn two ways (the row picture and the column picture), and the pictures show whether there is one answer, many, or none.

7. How this is used with real options

  • Every product is an arrow. Its payoff list (one number per Nifty level) is a vector. Seeing products as arrows is the column picture, and it's how quants think about hedging.
  • Buildable payoffs = everything the arrows can reach. If your arrows point in as many different directions as there are Nifty levels, any payoff can be built (a complete market). If they lie flat, only payoffs on their sheet can be built.
  • Every level must match. The row picture is a risk check: a hedge that works at 19,900 but misses at 20,100 still leaves you exposed. Only the meeting point is a full hedge.
  • Price and arbitrage: the recipe's cost is lots × premiums. If two different recipes land on the same target but cost different amounts, there's arbitrage.
  • Two levels (2D): start there. Lines instead of sheets, and flat arrows in a plane, make the ideas easy to see before going to 3D.

Example 5: No solution (singular)

Problem $$ x + y = 1, \qquad 2x + 2y = 6 $$
Words used here

Slope = how steep a line is. Intercept = where a line crosses an axis. Parallel = same slope, never meeting. Singular = the columns point along one line, so they can't reach every $b$. Multiple of a vector = that vector times some number. Contradiction = two equations that can't both be true.

  1. What we do Divide the second equation, $2x + 2y = 6$, by 2 on both sides.

    Why To compare it with the first equation, which has $1x + 1y$.

    How $2x \div 2 = x$, $2y \div 2 = y$, $6 \div 2 = 3$. So $x + y = 3$.

    What we have now Equation 1 says $x + y = 1$; equation 2 says $x + y = 3$.

  2. What we do Ask whether both can be true.

    How The same sum $x + y$ can't be $1$ and $3$ at once: $1 \ne 3$.

    What we have now No $(x, y)$ works. Check with any try, e.g. $(1, 0)$: equation 1 gives $1 + 0 = 1$ ✓, but equation 2 gives $2 \times 1 + 2 \times 0 = 2 \ne 6$ ✗.

Row picture. Divide the second equation by 2: $x + y = 3$. So the two lines have the same slope but different intercepts. They are parallel and never meet.

Column picture. Column 1 = $(1, 2)$ and column 2 = $(1, 2)$ are the same vector. Every combination $x(1,2) + y(1,2) = (x+y)(1,2)$ lies on one line through the origin. $b = (1, 6)$ is not a multiple of $(1, 2)$, so it cannot be reached.

  1. What we do Try to write $b = (1, 6)$ as $c \times (1, 2)$.

    Why Every combination of the columns is some number $c = x + y$ times $(1, 2)$.

    How Top: $c \times 1 = 1$ forces $c = 1$. Bottom: $c \times 2 = 1 \times 2 = 2$, but $b$ needs $6$. $2 \ne 6$ ✗.

    What we have now No $c$ works, so $b$ is off the line of the columns.

Answer: no solution. The matrix is singular.

    What we have now Any $(x, y)$ with $x + y = 1$ works: infinitely many.

Column picture. Again both columns are $(1, 2)$. This time $b = (1, 2)$ does lie on that line, so it can be reached, and in many ways:

$$ (x, y) = (1, 0),\; (0, 1),\; (2, -1),\; \dots \quad\text{any } x + y = 1 $$

Answer: infinitely many solutions.

Compare Examples 5 and 6

Same singular matrix, different $b$. If $b$ lies in the space the columns can reach, there are infinitely many solutions. If it does not, there are none. A singular matrix never gives exactly one solution.

Example 7: Matrix × vector, both ways

Problem $$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \; ? $$
Words used here

By columns = $Ax$ is $x_1$ copies of column 1 plus $x_2$ copies of column 2 plus $x_3$ copies of column 3. By rows = each answer number is one row of $A$ dotted with $x$. Dot product = multiply matching entries, then add. Entry = one number in the matrix or vector.

By columns. $$ 1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} - 1\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + 2\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - 2 + 0 \\ 0 - 1 + 6 \\ 2 - 0 + 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 5 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

  1. What we do Take $1$ copy of column 1, $(1, 0, 2)$.

    How $(1 \times 1,\ 1 \times 0,\ 1 \times 2) = (1, 0, 2)$.

  2. What we do Take $-1$ copy of column 2, $(2, 1, 0)$.

    Why The second entry of $x$ is $-1$: use column 2 backwards.

    How $(-1 \times 2,\ -1 \times 1,\ -1 \times 0) = (-2, -1, 0)$.

  3. What we do Take $2$ copies of column 3, $(0, 3, 1)$.

    How $(2 \times 0,\ 2 \times 3,\ 2 \times 1) = (0, 6, 2)$.

  4. What we do Add the three pieces, row by row.

    How Top: $1 + (-2) + 0 = -1$. Middle: $0 + (-1) + 6 = 5$. Bottom: $2 + 0 + 2 = 4$.

    What we have now $Ax = (-1, 5, 4)$.

By rows. $(1,2,0)\cdot(1,-1,2) = -1$,   $(0,1,3)\cdot(1,-1,2) = 5$,   $(2,0,1)\cdot(1,-1,2) = 4$ ✓

  1. What we do Row 1 $(1, 2, 0)$ dotted with $x = (1, -1, 2)$.

    How $1 \times 1 = 1$; $2 \times (-1) = -2$; $0 \times 2 = 0$. Add: $1 - 2 + 0 = -1$.

  2. What we do Row 2 $(0, 1, 3)$ dotted with $x$.

    How $0 \times 1 = 0$; $1 \times (-1) = -1$; $3 \times 2 = 6$. Add: $0 - 1 + 6 = 5$.

  3. What we do Row 3 $(2, 0, 1)$ dotted with $x$.

    How $2 \times 1 = 2$; $0 \times (-1) = 0$; $1 \times 2 = 2$. Add: $2 + 0 + 2 = 4$.

    What we have now $(-1, 5, 4)$, the same as by columns ✓.

Answer: $(-1, 5, 4)$

Example 8: Is $b$ a combination of the columns?

Problem

Let $u = (1, 0, 1)$ and $v = (0, 1, 1)$. Is $b$ a combination of $u$ and $v$ when (a) $b = (2, 3, 5)$ and (b) $b = (1, 1, 1)$?

Words used here

Combination of $u$ and $v$ = $x\,u + y\,v$ for some numbers $x, y$. Component = one number of a vector (top, middle, bottom). Condition = an extra equation that may or may not hold. Span = every combination of $u$ and $v$; here a plane. Plane $z = x + y$ = all points whose third number is the first plus the second.

What the question means. "$b$ is a combination of $u$ and $v$" means we can find two numbers $x$ and $y$ with

$$ x\,u + y\,v = b. $$

So we look for $x$ and $y$. If they exist, the answer is yes. If no pair works, the answer is no.

Step 1. Work out $x\,u + y\,v$ in general.

  1. What we do Scale $u = (1, 0, 1)$ by $x$.

    How $(x \times 1,\ x \times 0,\ x \times 1) = (x, 0, x)$.

  2. What we do Scale $v = (0, 1, 1)$ by $y$.

    How $(y \times 0,\ y \times 1,\ y \times 1) = (0, y, y)$.

  3. What we do Add, component by component.

    How Top: $x + 0 = x$. Middle: $0 + y = y$. Bottom: $x + y$.

    $$ x \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + y \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ 0 \\ x \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ y \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x + 0 \\ 0 + y \\ x + y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ x + y \end{bmatrix} $$

    What we have now Every combination looks like $(x,\ y,\ x + y)$.

Every combination of $u$ and $v$ has this shape: the third component is always the first plus the second.

Step 2. Set it equal to $b = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$. Matching components gives three equations:

$$ \begin{aligned} x &= b_1 &&\text{(top)} \\ y &= b_2 &&\text{(middle)} \\ x + y &= b_3 &&\text{(bottom)} \end{aligned} $$

There are 3 equations but only 2 unknowns. The top and middle equations already fix $x$ and $y$. The bottom equation is then an extra condition that may or may not hold.

(a) $b = (2, 3, 5)$

  1. What we do Read $x$ from the top equation and $y$ from the middle one.

    Why The top says $x = b_1$ and the middle says $y = b_2$: they hand us the answers directly.

    How $b_1 = 2$, so $x = 2$. $b_2 = 3$, so $y = 3$.

    What we have now $x = 2$, $y = 3$, with no choice left.

  2. What we do Test the bottom equation, $x + y = b_3$.

    How $2 + 3 = 5$, and $b_3 = 5$. Equal ✓.

    $$ \begin{aligned} x &= 2 &&\text{top: } b_1 = 2 \\ y &= 3 &&\text{middle: } b_2 = 3 \\ x + y &= 2 + 3 = 5 &&\text{bottom needs } b_3 = 5 \;✓ \end{aligned} $$

    What we have now All three equations hold.

  3. What we do Check by building $2u + 3v$.

    How $2u = (2, 0, 2)$. $3v = (0, 3, 3)$. Add: top $2 + 0 = 2$, middle $0 + 3 = 3$, bottom $2 + 3 = 5$.

    $$ 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 3 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = b \;✓ $$

(a) Yes: $b = 2u + 3v$.

(b) $b = (1, 1, 1)$

  1. What we do Read $x$ and $y$ from the top and middle equations.

    How $b_1 = 1$, so $x = 1$. $b_2 = 1$, so $y = 1$.

    What we have now $x = 1$, $y = 1$, forced: no freedom left.

  2. What we do Test the bottom equation, $x + y = b_3$.

    How $1 + 1 = 2$, but $b_3 = 1$, and $2 \ne 1$ ✗.

    $$ \begin{aligned} x &= 1 &&\text{top: } b_1 = 1 \\ y &= 1 &&\text{middle: } b_2 = 1 \\ x + y &= 1 + 1 = 2 &&\text{bottom needs } b_3 = 1, \text{ but } 2 \ne 1 \;✗ \end{aligned} $$

    What we have now The only possible $x, y$ fail the third equation.

  3. What we do Build the forced combination $1u + 1v$ to see what it really gives.

    How Top: $1 + 0 = 1$. Middle: $0 + 1 = 1$. Bottom: $1 + 1 = 2$.

    $$ 1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 1 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \ne \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

    What we have now $(1, 1, 2)$, which misses $b = (1, 1, 1)$ in the bottom number.

It misses $b$ by $1$ in the third component, and no other choice of $x, y$ can fix that.

(b) No: $(1, 1, 1)$ is not a combination of $u$ and $v$.

The picture: two vectors fill only a plane

All combinations have the form $(x,\ y,\ x + y)$, so they all lie on the flat plane $z = x + y$. That plane is the span of $u$ and $v$.

  • $(2, 3, 5)$: $2 + 3 = 5$, so it is on the plane and can be reached.
  • $(1, 1, 1)$: $1 + 1 = 2 \ne 1$, so it is below the plane (by $1$) and can never be reached.

Two vectors can never fill all of 3D space. Most points in 3D are off their plane.

In matrix form

This is the system $Ax = b$ with the $3 \times 2$ matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (columns $u$ and $v$). With more equations than unknowns, $Ax = b$ is solvable only for the $b$ that lie in the plane of the columns.

Example 9: Building a payoff from two assets (finance)

Problem

You buy assets today. Tomorrow the market goes either up or down, and each asset pays you money depending on which happens.

AssetPrice today (you pay)Pays tomorrow if upPays tomorrow if down
Bond$1$$1$$1$
Stock$2$$3$$1$

You want to receive $5$ if up and $3$ if down. How many bonds and how many stocks should you buy, and what does it cost today?

Words used here

Asset = something you can buy (here a bond or a stock). Bond = pays the same amount whatever happens. Stock = pays more if the market goes up, less if down. State = one possible tomorrow (up or down). Price today = what you pay now. Payoff tomorrow = what you receive later, depending on the state. $x$ = number of bonds, $y$ = number of stocks. Column = one asset's payoffs (up on top, down below). Replicate = build the wanted payoff from assets.

First, the same problem as an everyday story

A shop sells two kinds of ticket. You buy them today. Tomorrow the weather is either ☀️ sunny or 🌧️ rainy, and each ticket pays you depending on the weather.

TicketYou pay todayPays tomorrow if ☀️ sunnyPays tomorrow if 🌧️ rainy
Ticket A (safe)$1$$1$$1$
Ticket B (risky)$2$$3$$1$
  • Ticket A is boring: pay $1$ today, get $1$ back tomorrow, whatever the weather.
  • Ticket B costs $2$ today. It pays a lot ($3$) if sunny but only $1$ if rainy.

Goal: tomorrow you want $5$ if sunny and $3$ if rainy. The answer is to buy 2 of Ticket A and 1 of Ticket B:

📅 Today, you payCost
2 × Ticket A$2 \times 1 = 2$
1 × Ticket B$1 \times 2 = 2$
Total paid today$4$
📅 Tomorrow, you receiveIf ☀️ sunnyIf 🌧️ rainy
2 × Ticket A$2 \times 1 = 2$$2 \times 1 = 2$
1 × Ticket B$1 \times 3 = 3$$1 \times 1 = 1$
Total received$5$ ✓$3$ ✓

Now replace the words: sunny/rainy = market up/down, Ticket A = bond, Ticket B = stock. That is exactly the problem above. The steps below show how "2 and 1" is found.

Step 1. Write it as columns. Let $x$ = number of bonds and $y$ = number of stocks. Use only what each asset pays tomorrow (up on top, down below). Prices are not needed yet.

$$ x \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{bond}} + y \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{stock}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{what you want}} \begin{matrix} \leftarrow \text{up} \\ \leftarrow \text{down} \end{matrix} $$

Step 2. One equation per state.

$$ \begin{aligned} x + 3y &= 5 &&\text{(up)} \\ x + y &= 3 &&\text{(down)} \end{aligned} $$

Step 3. Solve. Subtract (down) from (up), and $x$ disappears:

  1. What we do Take the (down) equation away from the (up) equation.

    Why Both have exactly one $x$. Subtracting makes $x$ vanish, leaving only $y$.

    How Left: $(x + 3y) - (x + y) = x - x + 3y - y = 0 + 2y = 2y$. Right: $5 - 3 = 2$.

    What we have now $2y = 2$.

  2. What we do Solve $2y = 2$.

    How Divide both sides by 2: $y = 2 \div 2 = 1$.

    $$ \begin{aligned} (x + 3y) - (x + y) &= 5 - 3 \\ 2y &= 2 \\ y &= 1 \end{aligned} $$

    What we have now $y = 1$: one stock.

  3. What we do Put $y = 1$ into (down), $x + y = 3$.

    How $x + 1 = 3$. Take 1 from both sides: $x = 3 - 1 = 2$.

    $$ \begin{aligned} x + 1 &= 3 \\ x &= 2 \end{aligned} $$

    What we have now $x = 2$: two bonds.

Step 4. Check what you receive tomorrow (the original equations, state by state).

  1. Check up $x + 3y = 2 + 3 \times 1 = 2 + 3 = 5$. Wanted $5$ ✓.

    Check down $x + y = 2 + 1 = 3$. Wanted $3$ ✓.

    State2 bonds1 stockTotalWanted
    Up$2 \times 1 = 2$$1 \times 3 = 3$$5$$5$ ✓
    Down$2 \times 1 = 2$$1 \times 1 = 1$$3$$3$ ✓

Answer: buy 2 bonds and 1 stock.

Step 5. What it costs today. Now use the prices:

  1. What we do Price each part with today's prices (bond $1$, stock $2$).

    Why What you pay today is price × how many, added up. Tomorrow's payoffs don't enter here.

    How 2 bonds: $2 \times 1 = 2$. 1 stock: $1 \times 2 = 2$. Total: $2 + 2 = 4$.

    $$ \underbrace{2 \times 1}_{\text{2 bonds}} + \underbrace{1 \times 2}_{\text{1 stock}} = 2 + 2 = 4 $$

    What we have now The recipe costs $4$ today.

You pay $4$ today, and tomorrow you receive $5$ (if up) or $3$ (if down).

This is the basic idea behind pricing options (Example 10): build the payoff from assets you know, then add up their prices.

This is the column picture. The assets' payoffs are the columns, the wanted payoff is $b$, and "how many to buy" are the weights $x$ and $y$. Because the bond and the stock point in different directions, any payoff you want can be built this way.

Example 10: Pricing a call option (finance)

Problem

Same market as Example 9. Tomorrow is either up or down, and today a bond costs $1$ and a stock costs $2$:

AssetPrice todayPays if upPays if down
Bond$1$$1$$1$
Stock$2$$3$$1$

A call option with strike $1$ gives you the right (not the obligation) to buy the stock tomorrow for $1$. What is the option worth today?

Words used here

Call option (like a CE) = the right, but not the duty, to buy something later at a fixed price. Strike = that fixed price (here $1$). Payoff = what the option gives you tomorrow: $\max(\text{stock} - \text{strike}, 0)$. Replicate = build the same payoff from bond and stock. Borrow = a negative number of bonds: get money now, repay it tomorrow. Price today = what the recipe costs now. Arbitrage = risk-free profit if two things that pay the same have different prices.

Step 1. Work out what the option pays tomorrow. You only use the option if the stock is worth more than $1$. Then you gain "stock price $- 1$".

StateStock priceUse the option?Option pays
Up$3$Yes: buy for $1$ something worth $3$$3 - 1 = 2$
Down$1$No gain: buy for $1$ something worth $1$$0$

  1. What we do Work out the payoff if the market goes up.

    How Stock $= 3$, strike $= 1$. $3 - 1 = 2$, which is more than $0$, so you use the option and gain $\max(2, 0) = 2$.

  2. What we do Work out the payoff if the market goes down.

    How Stock $= 1$, strike $= 1$. $1 - 1 = 0$, so $\max(0, 0) = 0$: nothing gained.

    What we have now The option's payoff vector is $(2, 0)$.

So the option pays $(2, 0)$: $2$ if up, $0$ if down.

Step 2. Build the same payoff from bond and stock (column picture). Find $x$ bonds and $y$ stocks with

$$ x \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{bond}} + y \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{stock}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{option}} \begin{matrix} \leftarrow \text{up} \\ \leftarrow \text{down} \end{matrix} $$

Step 3. One equation per state.

$$ \begin{aligned} x + 3y &= 2 &&\text{(up)} \\ x + y &= 0 &&\text{(down)} \end{aligned} $$

Step 4. Solve. Subtract (down) from (up):

  1. What we do Take (down) away from (up).

    Why Each has one $x$, so subtracting removes $x$.

    How Left: $(x + 3y) - (x + y) = x - x + 3y - y = 2y$. Right: $2 - 0 = 2$.

    What we have now $2y = 2$.

  2. What we do Solve $2y = 2$.

    How Divide both sides by 2: $y = 1$.

    $$ \begin{aligned} (x + 3y) - (x + y) &= 2 - 0 \\ 2y &= 2 \\ y &= 1 \end{aligned} $$

    What we have now $y = 1$: one stock.

  3. What we do Put $y = 1$ into (down), $x + y = 0$.

    How $x + 1 = 0$. Take 1 from both sides: $x = 0 - 1 = -1$.

    $$ \begin{aligned} x + 1 &= 0 \\ x &= -1 \end{aligned} $$

    What we have now $x = -1$: minus one bond.

Step 5. Check in the original equations, state by state.

  1. Check up $x + 3y = -1 + 3 \times 1 = -1 + 3 = 2$. Option pays $2$ ✓.

    Check down $x + y = -1 + 1 = 0$. Option pays $0$ ✓.

    State$-1$ bond$1$ stockTotalOption pays
    Up$-1 \times 1 = -1$$1 \times 3 = 3$$2$$2$ ✓
    Down$-1 \times 1 = -1$$1 \times 1 = 1$$0$$0$ ✓

Step 6. What $x = -1$ means. A negative number of bonds means you borrow instead of buy: you get $1$ today and must pay back $1$ tomorrow, whatever happens. So the recipe is

Buy 1 stock and borrow 1. This pays exactly the same as the option.

Step 7. Price the option. The recipe costs

  1. What we do Add up today's cost of the recipe.

    Why Same payoff tomorrow means same price today; otherwise you could buy the cheap one, sell the dear one, and pocket the gap with no risk.

    How Borrowing 1 bond: $-1 \times 1 = -1$ (you receive 1). Buying 1 stock: $1 \times 2 = 2$ (you pay 2). Net: $-1 + 2 = 1$.

    $$ \underbrace{-1 \times 1}_{\text{borrow 1}} + \underbrace{1 \times 2}_{\text{buy 1 stock}} = -1 + 2 = 1. $$

    What we have now The recipe costs $1$, so the option is worth $1$.

The option and the recipe pay the same tomorrow in every state, so they must cost the same today.

The option is worth $1$ today.

The idea in one line

To price an option: write its payoff as a combination of assets whose prices you know (solve $Ax = b$), then add up the cost of that combination. If the option sold for any other price, you could make risk-free money by buying the cheaper one and selling the dearer one.

1. In the lecture's 2 × 2 example, which line goes through the origin, and why?

$2x - y = 0$, because its right-hand side is $0$, so $(0,0)$ satisfies it. The line $-x + 2y = 3$ does not, since $(0,0)$ gives $0 \ne 3$.

2. For the lecture's 3 × 3 matrix $A$, solve $Ax = (2, -1, 0)$ without any computation.

$(2, -1, 0)$ is column 1, so $x = (1, 0, 0)$.

3. Columns $(1,0,1)$, $(0,1,1)$, $(1,1,2)$: can their combinations reach every $b$ in 3D?

No. Column 3 = column 1 + column 2, so all three columns lie in one plane. The matrix is singular, and only $b$ in that plane can be reached.

4. Compute $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$ by columns.

$2(1, 3) - 1(2, 4) = (2 - 2,\; 6 - 4) = (0, 2)$.

  1. What we do Take 2 copies of column 1, $(1, 3)$.

    How $(2 \times 1,\ 2 \times 3) = (2, 6)$.

  2. What we do Take $-1$ copy of column 2, $(2, 4)$.

    How $(-1 \times 2,\ -1 \times 4) = (-2, -4)$.

  3. What we do Add box by box.

    How Top: $2 + (-2) = 0$. Bottom: $6 + (-4) = 2$.

    Check by rows Row 1: $1 \times 2 + 2 \times (-1) = 2 - 2 = 0$ ✓. Row 2: $3 \times 2 + 4 \times (-1) = 6 - 4 = 2$ ✓.

5. For which $c$ is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & c \end{bmatrix}$ singular?

It is singular when column 2 is a multiple of column 1: $(2, c) = 2(1, 3)$, so $c = 6$.

6. Write $3x - y = 5,\; x + 4y = 6$ in column form and solve it.

$x(3, 1) + y(-1, 4) = (5, 6)$. From the second equation $x = 6 - 4y$. Substituting gives $18 - 13y = 5$, so $y = 1$ and $x = 2$. Check: $2(3,1) + 1(-1,4) = (5, 6)$ ✓.

  1. What we do Get $x$ alone from $x + 4y = 6$.

    How Take $4y$ from both sides: $x = 6 - 4y$.

  2. What we do Put it into $3x - y = 5$.

    How, move 1 $3(6 - 4y) - y = 5$.

    How, move 2 Open the bracket: $3 \times 6 = 18$, $3 \times (-4y) = -12y$. So $18 - 12y - y = 5$.

    How, move 3 $-12y - y = -13y$: $18 - 13y = 5$.

    How, move 4 Take 18 from both sides: $-13y = 5 - 18 = -13$.

    How, move 5 Divide by $-13$: $y = 1$.

  3. What we do Put $y = 1$ into $x = 6 - 4y$.

    How $4 \times 1 = 4$, then $6 - 4 = 2$. So $x = 2$.
