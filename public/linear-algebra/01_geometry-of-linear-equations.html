Part 3 Explanation

The running example: a portfolio mix

You have ₹10 lakh to invest and two funds to choose from:

FundExpected return per yearReturn on ₹1 lakh
Nifty 50 index fund12%₹12 thousand
Government bond fund7%₹7 thousand

You want to invest all ₹10 lakh and earn ₹1,05,000 a year (₹105 thousand, a 10.5% return). How much goes into each fund?

The returns are illustrative round numbers, not forecasts.

1. The problem: $Ax = b$

Give the two amounts letters. These are the unknowns:

  • $x$ = ₹ lakh put in the Nifty fund,
  • $y$ = ₹ lakh put in the bond fund.

Now write each requirement as an equation:

  • Budget rule. The two amounts must add up to ₹10 lakh: $\;x + y = 10$.
  • Return rule. Each lakh in Nifty earns ₹12 thousand and each lakh in bonds earns ₹7 thousand, and together they must earn ₹105 thousand: $\;12x + 7y = 105$.
$$ \begin{aligned} x + y &= 10 &&\text{(budget, ₹ lakh)}\\ 12x + 7y &= 105 &&\text{(return, ₹ thousand)} \end{aligned} $$

Two equations that must both be true at once are a system of equations. A solution is a pair $(x, y)$ that passes both rules.

The numbers that multiply the unknowns (the $1, 1$ and $12, 7$) are coefficients.

Definition: linear equation

An equation is linear when each unknown is only multiplied by a number and the pieces are added. No squares, no unknowns multiplied together, no $\sqrt{x}$ or $\sin x$.

Linear ✓Not linear ✗
$x + y = 10$$x^2 + y = 10$ (a square)
$12x + 7y = 105$$xy = 21$ (unknowns multiplied together)

"Linear" has the word line in it: a linear equation in two unknowns draws a straight line.

The fundamental problem of linear algebra is to solve such a system: $n$ linear equations in $n$ unknowns. This lesson looks at one system in three ways.

ViewYou look at…The question becomes…
Row pictureone equation (one rule) at a timeWhere do the lines meet?
Column picture ★one unknown (one fund) at a timeWhat mix of the fund arrows makes the target?
Matrix formthe whole system at onceFind $x$ with $Ax = b$.

The star marks the column picture: it is the view the rest of the course is built on.

Check yourself: write the system if the target return were ₹1,00,000.

Only the right-hand side of the return rule changes: $x + y = 10$ and $12x + 7y = 100$.

2. Vectors and combinations

Look at what ₹1 lakh in the Nifty fund does to our two rules. It uses ₹1 lakh of the budget and earns ₹12 thousand of return. Write those two numbers as a column:

$$ \text{Nifty fund: } \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 12 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{Bond fund: } \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \leftarrow \begin{matrix} \text{budget used (₹ lakh)} \\ \text{return earned (₹ thousand)} \end{matrix}$$
Definition: vector

A vector is an ordered list of numbers, written as a column. Each number is a component. The Nifty vector $(1, 12)$ has components $1$ and $12$, and the order matters: first budget, then return.

A vector with 2 components can be drawn as an arrow from the origin: here, 1 step along the budget axis and 12 steps up the return axis.

The set of all vectors with 2 components is called $\mathbb{R}^2$ (say "R-two"). $\mathbb{R}$ means the real numbers, and the little 2 says how many components. Likewise $\mathbb{R}^3$ is all lists of 3 numbers (3D space) and $\mathbb{R}^n$ is all lists of $n$ numbers, which we can't draw once $n > 3$ but can still calculate with.

Two moves: scale and add

  • Scalar multiplication. Multiply every component by one number (a scalar). ₹7 lakh in Nifty is 7 copies of ₹1 lakh: $\;7\begin{bmatrix}1\\12\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}7\\84\end{bmatrix}$. Same direction, 7 times as long.
  • Vector addition. Add matching components. ₹1 lakh in each fund: $\;\begin{bmatrix}1\\12\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}1\\7\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}2\\19\end{bmatrix}$: ₹2 lakh used, ₹19 thousand earned. As arrows: walk the first arrow, then walk the second from where you stopped.
Definition: linear combination

Scale some vectors, then add them. For vectors $c, d$ and numbers $x, y$, the vector $\;x\,c + y\,d\;$ is a linear combination of $c$ and $d$. The numbers $x, y$ are its weights.

In our example a linear combination of the two fund vectors is simply a portfolio: $x$ lakh of Nifty plus $y$ lakh of bonds. Strang calls this "the most fundamental operation in the whole course".

Check yourself: what vector describes ₹4 lakh in Nifty plus ₹2 lakh in bonds?

$4\begin{bmatrix}1\\12\end{bmatrix} + 2\begin{bmatrix}1\\7\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}4\\48\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}2\\14\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}6\\62\end{bmatrix}$: ₹6 lakh used, ₹62 thousand earned.

3. Matrix form

Put all the coefficients in one table. A matrix is just a rectangular table of numbers:

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}}_{x} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 105 \end{bmatrix}}_{b} \qquad\text{or simply}\qquad Ax = b. $$
SymbolNameIn our example
$A$coefficient matrixrow $i$ = rule $i$ (budget, return); column $j$ = fund $j$ (Nifty, bond)
$x$vector of unknownsthe amounts $(x, y)$ in ₹ lakh
$b$right-hand sidethe targets $(10, 105)$

A matrix with $m$ rows and $n$ columns is called $m \times n$ ("$m$ by $n$"). Ours is $2 \times 2$: 2 rules, 2 funds. Notice the columns of $A$ are exactly the fund vectors from topic 2.

Watch out

The letter $x$ does two jobs: the single unknown $x$ (Nifty amount) and the whole vector of unknowns $x = (x, y)$. Strang writes the vector in bold. From the context it is always clear which one is meant.

What $Ax$ means: matrix × vector, two ways

Method · Matrix–vector multiplication

By columns: $Ax$ is a linear combination of the columns of $A$, with the entries of $x$ as weights. By rows: each entry of $Ax$ is the dot product of one row of $A$ with $x$. Same answer either way.

What does $Ax$ actually mean? Take our 2 × 2 matrix and the portfolio $x = (7, 3)$. There are two ways to multiply, and both give the same answer.

Way 1: by columns (Strang's favourite).

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = 7\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 12 \end{bmatrix} + 3\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 + 3 \\ 84 + 21 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 105 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Read it as "7 lakh of fund 1 plus 3 lakh of fund 2". This is the idea behind the column picture (topic 5).

Way 2: by rows (dot products). Multiply each row of $A$ by $x$, entry by entry, and add. This is a dot product:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\cdot 7 + 1\cdot 3 \\ 12\cdot 7 + 7\cdot 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 105 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Each row answers one rule: row 1 · $x$ = budget used, row 2 · $x$ = return earned. This is the idea behind the row picture (topic 4).

Key fact

$Ax$ is a combination of the columns of $A$, with the entries of $x$ as the weights. So "$Ax = b$ has a solution" means exactly "$b$ is a combination of the columns".

Size rule

An $m \times n$ matrix times a vector with $n$ entries gives a vector with $m$ entries. The number of columns of $A$ must equal the number of entries of $x$: one weight per column (one amount per fund).

More generally $(m \times n)\,(n \times p) = (m \times p)$: the inner sizes must match, the outer sizes give the answer's shape.

Check yourself: compute $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ both ways (Strang's example).

By columns: $1(2, 1) + 2(5, 3) = (2 + 10,\ 1 + 6) = (12, 7)$. By rows: $2\cdot1 + 5\cdot2 = 12$ and $1\cdot1 + 3\cdot2 = 7$. Same answer, $(12, 7)$.

Check yourself: true or false? A 3 × 2 matrix times a 2 × 3 matrix is a 3 × 3 matrix.

True. The inner sizes (2 and 2) match, and the outer sizes give $3 \times 3$.

4. The row picture

Method · Graphical method (row picture)

Draw each equation as a line (or a plane in 3D) and read off the point where they all meet. Great for seeing 2 unknowns; impractical beyond 3.

Idea: take one equation (one row) at a time and draw every point $(x, y)$ that satisfies it. A linear equation in two unknowns gives a straight line. A solution must satisfy both rules, so it sits on both lines: it is where the lines cross.

Draw each line from two points

Two points fix a line. An easy method: set one unknown to $0$ and solve for the other.

Budget: $x + y = 10$Return: $12x + 7y = 105$
Put $x = 0$$y = 10$ → point $(0, 10)$$7y = 105$, $y = 15$ → point $(0, 15)$
Put $y = 0$$x = 10$ → point $(10, 0)$$12x = 105$, $x = 8.75$ → point $(8.75, 0)$
Meaningall-bonds or all-Nifty, both use ₹10 lakh₹15 lakh in bonds, or ₹8.75 lakh in Nifty, each earns ₹105 thousand

Check the crossing point in both rules. Reading a point off a picture is not proof; the arithmetic is.

  • Budget: $7 + 3 = 10$ ✓
  • Return: $12 \times 7 + 7 \times 3 = 84 + 21 = 105$ ✓

So the answer is ₹7 lakh in the Nifty fund and ₹3 lakh in the bond fund.

Tip: does the line pass through the origin?

Strang always asks this first. A line passes through $(0, 0)$ exactly when its right-hand side is $0$. Neither of our lines does: investing nothing can't use ₹10 lakh or earn ₹105 thousand.

The row picture is the one you met in school, and it works well for two unknowns. With three unknowns each rule becomes a plane, and it gets hard to see (topic 7).

Check yourself: where does the budget line cross the return line if the target return is ₹1,20,000?

Solve $x + y = 10$ and $12x + 7y = 120$. From the first, $y = 10 - x$. Then $12x + 70 - 7x = 120$, so $5x = 50$, $x = 10$, $y = 0$: all ₹10 lakh in Nifty. The lines cross on the $x$-axis at $(10, 0)$.

5. The column picture ★

Method · Column picture (linear combination of columns)

Write $Ax = b$ as $x\,(\text{column } 1) + y\,(\text{column } 2) + \dots = b$ and look for the weights that land on $b$. The view the whole course builds on.

Now read the same system a column at a time. Pull out everything that multiplies $x$ and everything that multiplies $y$:

$$ x \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 12 \end{bmatrix} + y \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 105 \end{bmatrix} $$

Read it aloud: "$x$ lakh of Nifty plus $y$ lakh of bonds must give ₹10 lakh used and ₹105 thousand earned." The left side is a linear combination of the columns of $A$. So the question has become:

The column question

Which combination of the column vectors $(1, 12)$ and $(1, 7)$ produces the target vector $b = (10, 105)$?

We already know the answer from the row picture, $x = 7$ and $y = 3$, so draw that combination: 7 copies of the Nifty arrow, then 3 copies of the bond arrow from its tip.

Component by component: budget $7 + 3 = 10$, return $84 + 21 = 105$. Same answer as the row picture, seen a completely different way.

Try it: mix the two funds

Move the sliders. The blue arrow is your Nifty amount, the green arrow your bond amount, and the dot is where your portfolio lands. Can you hit the target ring?

Check yourself: in the column picture, what does the point $(10, 70)$ mean, and which portfolio reaches it?

₹10 lakh used and ₹70 thousand earned, a 7% return. Only bonds earn 7%, so $x = 0$, $y = 10$: $0\cdot(1, 12) + 10\cdot(1, 7) = (10, 70)$.

6. Which targets can we reach?

Strang now asks a bigger question: if we try every $x$ and every $y$, which right-hand sides $b$ can we produce? In money terms: which (budget, return) targets can some mix of these two funds hit?

Result

The combinations of $(1, 12)$ and $(1, 7)$ fill the whole plane $\mathbb{R}^2$. For every target $b$ there is exactly one mix $(x, y)$.

Why: the two arrows point in different directions. One arrow alone only reaches points on its own line (see the scaling figure in topic 2). A second arrow pointing a different way lets you move off that line, and with both you can reach anywhere.

Why it works: solve for any target $(B, R)$

Take any budget $B$ and any return $R$. We need $x + y = B$ and $12x + 7y = R$.

From the first, $y = B - x$. Put that into the second: $12x + 7(B - x) = R$, so $5x + 7B = R$, giving

$$ x = \frac{R - 7B}{5}, \qquad y = B - x = \frac{12B - R}{5}. $$

We only divided by $5 = 12 - 7$, which is not zero because the two funds have different returns. So a solution exists for every $(B, R)$, and it is the only one. If the returns were equal we would be dividing by zero: that is the singular case in topic 8.

Definition: span

The span of some vectors is the set of every point you can reach by mixing them: scale each one by any number (positive, negative or zero) and add. In portfolio words: every (budget, return) result you can get from these funds, in any amounts.

  • Vectors = your available directions (here, the funds).
  • Span = everywhere you can reach using those directions.

It is not about one target. It is about the whole reachable area: a line, a plane, or a whole space.

See how the span grows as we add funds:

Funds you may useTheir spanWhat it means
Nifty only, $(1, 12)$a line through the origin: return $= 12 \times$ budgetwhatever you invest, you earn exactly 12%
Bonds only, $(1, 7)$a different line: return $= 7 \times$ budgetyou earn exactly 7%
Nifty and bondsthe whole plane $\mathbb{R}^2$any budget with any return (using negative amounts if needed)
Definition: column space

The column space of a matrix $A$ is the span of its columns. Strang writes it $C(A)$. For us the columns are the funds, so $C(A)$ is every target your funds can produce.

For our matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$, the column space is all of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

Why the column space matters

$Ax = b$ has a solution exactly when $b$ is in the column space of $A$.

The reason is one sentence: $Ax$ is always a combination of the columns of $A$ (see topic 3), so asking "is there an $x$ with $Ax = b$?" is the same as asking "is $b$ one of those combinations?".

  • Nifty + bonds: $C(A)$ is the whole plane, so every target, including $(10, 105)$, has a solution.
  • Two Nifty funds (topic 8): $C(A)$ is only the line return $= 12 \times$ budget. The target $(10, 120)$ is on it (solvable); $(10, 105)$ is not (no solution).
Watch out: maths says yes, the market may say no

"Every target" includes strange ones. Want a 15% return on ₹10 lakh, ₹150 thousand? The formula gives $x = (150 - 70)/5 = 16$ and $y = (120 - 150)/5 = -6$. A negative amount means borrowing ₹6 lakh at the bond rate to put ₹16 lakh into Nifty. The algebra is happy; whether you are allowed to (or should) do that is a separate question.

Check yourself: which mix earns exactly 10% (₹100 thousand) on ₹10 lakh?

$x = (100 - 70)/5 = 6$ and $y = (120 - 100)/5 = 4$. Check: $6 + 4 = 10$ ✓ and $72 + 28 = 100$ ✓.

7. Three funds, three rules: 3 × 3

Add a third fund and a third rule.

  • Midcap fund: expected 15% a year (₹15 thousand per lakh), but riskier.
  • Market exposure rule. A fund's beta says how much it moves when Nifty moves 1%. Nifty fund: beta $1.0$. Bond fund: beta $0$. Midcap fund: beta $1.2$. Each lakh of a fund adds "beta × 1 lakh" of Nifty-like exposure, and you want ₹7.4 lakh of exposure in total.

With $z$ = ₹ lakh in the midcap fund:

$$ \begin{aligned} x + y + z &= 10 &&\text{(1) budget}\\ 12x + 7y + 15z &= 111 &&\text{(2) return}\\ 1.0x \phantom{{}+7y} + 1.2z &= 7.4 &&\text{(3) market exposure} \end{aligned} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 & 15 \\ 1 & 0 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 111 \\ 7.4 \end{bmatrix}}_{b} $$

Now $A$ is $3 \times 3$. Each column is still one fund: (budget used, return earned, exposure added) per ₹1 lakh. The bond fund's column has a $0$ at the bottom because it adds no market exposure.

Solve it step by step

Method · Substitution

Solve one equation for one unknown, put that expression into the other equations, and repeat until one unknown is left. Fine by hand for small systems; Gaussian elimination (Lesson 2) is the systematic version every computer uses.

A systematic method, elimination, is Lesson 2. Here, substitution is enough: each step removes one unknown.

  1. Equation (3) has no $y$. Write $x$ in terms of $z$: $\;x = 7.4 - 1.2z$.
  2. Equation (1) gives $y$. $y = 10 - x - z = 10 - (7.4 - 1.2z) - z = 2.6 + 0.2z$.
  3. Put both into equation (2). $12(7.4 - 1.2z) + 7(2.6 + 0.2z) + 15z = 111$. Multiply out: $88.8 - 14.4z + 18.2 + 1.4z + 15z = 111$, so $107 + 2z = 111$ and $z = 2$.
  4. Back-substitute. $x = 7.4 - 2.4 = 5$ and $y = 2.6 + 0.4 = 3$.

Check: budget $5 + 3 + 2 = 10$ ✓; return $60 + 21 + 30 = 111$ ✓; exposure $5 + 0 + 2.4 = 7.4$ ✓. So ₹5 lakh Nifty, ₹3 lakh bonds, ₹2 lakh midcap.

Row picture: three planes

With three unknowns, all the points $(x, y, z)$ satisfying one linear equation form a flat plane. Two planes usually meet in a line; the third plane cuts that line at one point, the solution.

Column picture: three arrows in 3D

$$ x\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 12 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + y\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 7 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + z\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 15 \\ 1.2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 111 \\ 7.4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Same question as before, one dimension up: which mix of the three fund arrows lands on $b$? Walk 5 Nifty arrows, then 3 bond arrows, then 2 midcap arrows.

Change only the target

Keep the same three funds (same $A$) but change the goal to ₹98 thousand return and ₹5.2 lakh exposure: $b = (10, 98, 5.2)$. The answer is $(4, 5, 1)$.

  • Check: $4 + 5 + 1 = 10$ ✓; $48 + 35 + 15 = 98$ ✓; $4 + 0 + 1.2 = 5.2$ ✓.
  • Row picture: three new planes (each right-hand side moved), now meeting at $(4, 5, 1)$.
  • Column picture: the same three arrows, just mixed in different amounts. This is why Strang prefers columns: the arrows belong to $A$ and never change; only the target does.
Check yourself: which mix of the three funds gives $b = (1, 15, 1.2)$?

$b$ is exactly the midcap column, so $x = 0$, $y = 0$, $z = 1$: ₹1 lakh in midcap. Strang uses the same trick in the lecture (his right-hand side equals column 3).

8. When it goes wrong: singular matrices

So far every target could be reached. Strang's key question:

The question, in two languages

Algebra: can we solve $Ax = b$ for every right-hand side $b$?

Columns: do the linear combinations of the columns fill the whole space?

These are the same question.

2 × 2: two funds that are really one

Replace the bond fund with a second Nifty 50 index fund from another company. It also earns 12%, so its column is also $(1, 12)$.

$$ x + y = 10, \qquad 12x + 12y = 105. $$

With these two funds you earn 12% whatever you do. If the target is ₹105 thousand there is no solution. If the target were exactly ₹120 thousand, every split works: the two lines would be the same line, giving infinitely many solutions.

Definition: singular and invertible

A square matrix is singular when its columns fill less than the whole space, so some $b$ cannot be reached. Its columns are dependent: one column adds nothing new.

Otherwise it is non-singular, also called invertible: the columns fill the whole space and every $b$ has exactly one solution. Our Nifty + bond matrix is invertible; the two-Nifty matrix is singular.

What happens (2 × 2)Row pictureColumn pictureNumber of solutions
Columns point different wayslines cross oncearrows fill the planeexactly one
Columns on one line, $b$ off itparallel lines$b$ off the arrows' linenone
Columns on one line, $b$ on itthe same line twice$b$ on the arrows' lineinfinitely many

3 × 3: a fund that is just a mix of the others

Replace the midcap fund with a balanced fund that simply holds half Nifty, half bonds. Per lakh it uses ₹1 lakh, earns $\tfrac12(12) + \tfrac12(7) = 9.5$ thousand and adds $\tfrac12(1) + \tfrac12(0) = 0.5$ lakh of exposure:

$$ \text{balanced} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 9.5 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix} = \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 12 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 7 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. $$

The third column is a combination of the first two, so all three columns lie in one plane. Every portfolio you can build lies in that plane too, and any target off it is unreachable. This is exactly Strang's warning: "if these three columns all lie in the same plane, then their combinations will lie in that same plane".

Watch out

Three funds do not guarantee three independent directions. A fund built from the others adds no new direction, however different its name or factsheet looks.

With many funds

Nothing changes with 9 funds and 9 rules: each column is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^9$ and the question is still "which mix of the columns reaches $b$?". You can't draw it, but the column picture still works. Funds picked at random almost always fill the whole space (invertible). Two copies of the same Nifty 50 index from different companies don't: the columns then fill only an 8-dimensional "plane" inside 9-dimensional space, and most targets are out of reach.

Check yourself: is the target $(10, 111, 7.4)$ reachable with Nifty, bond and balanced funds?

Every reachable target $(B, R, E)$ satisfies $-7B + R - 5E = 0$ (the equation of the plane; each column passes, e.g. Nifty: $-7 + 12 - 5 = 0$). For $(10, 111, 7.4)$: $-70 + 111 - 37 = 4 \ne 0$. So no: it is off the plane.

9. Summary

Key ideas

  1. A linear system is $Ax = b$: the matrix $A$ (rows = rules, columns = funds) times the unknowns $x$ equals the targets $b$. (topic 3)
  2. $Ax$ is a combination of the columns of $A$; it can also be computed by rows with dot products. (topic 3)
  3. Row picture: each equation is a line (2D) or a plane (3D); the solution is where they all meet. (topic 4)
  4. Column picture ★: $b$ is a linear combination of the columns; the solution gives the amounts. ₹7 lakh Nifty + ₹3 lakh bonds = (₹10 lakh, ₹105 thousand). (topic 5)
  5. Span: vectors are your available directions; the span is everywhere they can reach. The column space $C(A)$ is the span of the columns, and $Ax = b$ is solvable exactly when $b$ is in it. (topic 6)
  6. The same ideas work for 3 × 3: three planes meet at $(5, 3, 2)$, and three fund arrows mix to reach $b$. (topic 7)
  7. If a column adds no new direction (a twin fund, a 50/50 balanced fund), $A$ is singular: some targets have no solution, others infinitely many. Otherwise $A$ is invertible. (topic 8)

Example 1: Hedging risk exposures

The situation

Your portfolio gains ₹8 thousand when Nifty rises 1% (market exposure 8) and ₹3 thousand when the dollar rises 1% against the rupee (currency exposure 3). You want to cancel both risks by selling futures. Each unit you sell removes its own exposures:

Hedge (per unit sold)Removes market exposureRemoves currency exposureWhy
Nifty futures10moves with the market only
Nifty IT futures11IT companies earn in dollars, so they also gain when the dollar rises

Exposure units are illustrative, not real contract sizes.

2 × 2: set it up

Let $x$ = units of Nifty futures sold and $y$ = units of Nifty IT futures sold. Each risk gives one rule:

$$ \begin{aligned} x + y &= 8 &&\text{(market)}\\ y &= 3 &&\text{(currency)} \end{aligned} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{A}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{b} $$

Rows of $A$ are the risks; columns are the hedges. Each column is a hedge's risk profile: $(1, 0)$ for Nifty futures, $(1, 1)$ for Nifty IT futures.

Check: market $5 + 3 = 8$ ✓; currency $0 + 3 = 3$ ✓. So sell 5 Nifty futures and 3 Nifty IT futures. Notice the order the pictures suggest: only the IT futures touch currency risk, so they are fixed first ($y = 3$), and Nifty futures mop up the remaining market risk.

3 × 3: add interest-rate risk

Suppose the portfolio also loses when interest rates move: rate exposure 2, and market exposure is now 10. Add a third hedge, Bank Nifty futures, which remove market and rate exposure $(1, 0, 1)$ (banks move with the market and with rates). With $z$ = units of Bank Nifty futures sold:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \quad \begin{matrix} \text{market} \\ \text{currency} \\ \text{rates} \end{matrix} $$

Row 2 says $y = 3$, row 3 says $z = 2$, and row 1 then gives $x = 10 - 3 - 2 = 5$. Sell 5 Nifty, 3 Nifty IT and 2 Bank Nifty futures.

Singular: two hedges with the same profile

Replace Nifty IT futures with a Nifty ETF sold short. It removes market risk only, $(1, 0)$, exactly like Nifty futures. Now

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \text{currency rule: } 0x + 0y = 3. $$

The currency rule has no solution at all: no amount of Nifty products cancels dollar risk. The matrix is singular because its two columns are the same direction. To hedge currency you need a hedge that points in a new direction, such as Nifty IT or USD/INR futures.

Check yourself: with Nifty and Nifty IT futures, what hedge cancels market exposure 6 and currency exposure 6?

$y = 6$ from the currency rule, then $x = 6 - 6 = 0$: sell 6 Nifty IT futures and no Nifty futures. IT futures alone carry exactly the profile $(1, 1)$, so 6 of them give $(6, 6)$.

Example 2: Lot sizes: building a payoff

The situation

A client wants a particular payoff at expiry: an amount that depends on where Nifty ends. You build it from products you can trade, and the question is how many lots of each. All payoffs are in Nifty points per lot, at expiry, before premiums.

Product (1 lot)Payoff rule at expiryIf Nifty ends at 19,800If Nifty ends at 20,200
Nifty futures bought at 20,000$S - 20{,}000$$-200$$+200$
20,000 call (CE)$\max(S - 20{,}000,\ 0)$$0$$+200$

We count lots and keep payoffs in points. To get rupees, multiply by the exchange's lot size; the number of lots doesn't change, so the answer never goes out of date.

2 × 2: set it up

The client wants −200 points if Nifty ends at 19,800 and +600 points if it ends at 20,200. Let $x$ = lots of futures and $y$ = lots of calls. Each scenario gives one rule:

$$ \begin{aligned} -200x + 0y &= -200 &&\text{(Nifty at 19,800)}\\ 200x + 200y &= 600 &&\text{(Nifty at 20,200)} \end{aligned} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \begin{bmatrix} -200 & 0 \\ 200 & 200 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -200 \\ 600 \end{bmatrix} $$

Here rows are scenarios and columns are products: each column is one product's payoff in every scenario.

Check: at 19,800, $-200 + 0 = -200$ ✓; at 20,200, $200 + 2 \times 200 = 600$ ✓. So buy 1 lot of futures and 2 lots of the 20,000 call.

3 × 3: three scenarios, three products

Now the client cares about three expiry levels: 19,800, 20,000 and 20,200, and wants payoffs $(-200, 100, 700)$. Use futures, a 19,900 call and a 20,100 call:

Nifty at expiryFutures19,900 call20,100 callTarget
19,800$-200$$0$$0$$-200$
20,000$0$$100$$0$$100$
20,200$200$$300$$100$$700$

Read the table as $Ax = b$: the three product columns are the columns of $A$. Row by row: 19,800 gives $-200x = -200$, so $x = 1$. 20,000 gives $100y = 100$, so $y = 1$. 20,200 gives $200 + 300 + 100z = 700$, so $z = 2$. 1 lot of futures, 1 lot of the 19,900 call, 2 lots of the 20,100 call.

Singular: futures, call and put at the same strike

Try futures, a 20,000 call and a 20,000 put (PE, pays $\max(20{,}000 - S,\ 0)$):

Nifty at expiryFutures20,000 call20,000 put
19,800$-200$$0$$200$
20,000$0$$0$$0$
20,200$200$$200$$0$

The futures column equals call minus put: $(-200, 0, 200) = (0, 0, 200) - (200, 0, 0)$. This is put–call parity at expiry, and it makes the matrix singular: the three columns lie in one plane, so they cannot reach every payoff. You can see it directly in the middle row: at 20,000 all three products pay $0$, so any payoff that is non-zero at 20,000 is impossible with these products.

Watch out

Three products are not automatically three directions. Options at the same strike plus futures are linked by parity; to shape payoffs at more levels you need different strikes, as in the 3 × 3 above.

Check yourself: in the 2 × 2, which lots give payoff $(0, 400)$?

The 19,800 rule gives $-200x = 0$, so $x = 0$. Then $200y = 400$, so $y = 2$: just 2 call lots. That is exactly 2 × the call column.
