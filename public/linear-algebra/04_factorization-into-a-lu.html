Part 3 Explanation

The running example: the portfolio, factored once

In Lesson 2 we eliminated the three-fund portfolio and reached $U$:

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 & 15 \\ 1 & 0 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \xrightarrow{\;\ell_{21} = 12,\;\; \ell_{31} = 1,\;\; \ell_{32} = 0.2\;} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & -0.4 \end{bmatrix}}_{U} $$

Now think like a fund desk: the funds and rules stay the same ($A$ is fixed) but clients bring different targets every day: ₹10 lakh at 11.1%, ₹10 lakh at 9.8%, ₹15 lakh... Redoing elimination for each is wasteful. This lesson records elimination once, as $A = LU$, and reuses it.

1. Inverse of a product

Rule · Inverse and transpose of a product

$(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ and $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$: reverse the order. Also $(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1}$.

Strang starts with one basic fact, because $L$ will turn out to be a product of inverses.

Inverse of a product

If $A$ and $B$ are invertible, then $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$: the same inverses, in reverse order.

Check it: $(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A\,(BB^{-1})\,A^{-1} = A\,I\,A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I$. We only moved parentheses (Lesson 2), and the middle pair cancelled first.

Shoes and socks

You put on socks, then shoes. To undo it you take off the shoes first, then the socks. Undoing always goes in reverse order. In elimination: we applied $E_{21}$, then $E_{31}$, then $E_{32}$; to undo them we apply $E_{32}^{-1}$ first and $E_{21}^{-1}$ last.

The transpose $A^T$ swaps rows and columns: the entry in row $i$, column $j$ of $A$ is in row $j$, column $i$ of $A^T$. For our two-fund matrix:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \qquad A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 12 \\ 1 & 7 \end{bmatrix} $$

In $A$ each column is a fund; in $A^T$ each row is a fund. Transposes follow the same reverse-order rule, $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$, and for an invertible matrix $(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1}$: you may transpose and invert in either order. Transposes return in Lesson 5.

Check yourself: what is $(ABC)^{-1}$?

$C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1}$. Check: $ABC\,C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1}$ collapses from the middle outwards to $I$.

2. 2 × 2: from EA = U to A = LU

Method · LU decomposition (LU factorization)

Write $A = LU$: $L$ lower triangular with 1s on its diagonal and the elimination multipliers below; $U$ upper triangular with the pivots. It is Gaussian elimination, recorded. This form, with the 1s in $L$, is also called the Doolittle factorization (the Crout form puts the 1s in $U$ instead).

The two-fund portfolio took one elimination step ($\ell_{21} = 12$):

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -12 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{E_{21}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 \end{bmatrix}}_{U}. $$

That has the elimination matrix on the left of $A$. Strang wants $A$ alone on one side. Multiply both sides by $E_{21}^{-1}$, which adds back 12 × row 1 (Lesson 2):

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 12 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{L = E_{21}^{-1}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 \end{bmatrix}}_{U}. $$

Check: row 2 of $LU$ is $12 \times (1, 1) + 1 \times (0, -5) = (12, 7)$ ✓, the return rule.

A = LU

$U$ is upper triangular with the pivots on its diagonal. $L$ is lower triangular with 1s on its diagonal and the multipliers below it. "L" for lower, "U" for upper.

In words: the return rule = 12 × (budget rule) + (the eliminated rule $-5y = -15$). $L$ records how to rebuild each original rule from the rows of $U$.

Check yourself (Strang's example): factor $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 8 & 7 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$.

$\ell_{21} = 8/2 = 4$, so $U = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and $L = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Check: $4(2, 1) + (0, 3) = (8, 7)$ ✓.

3. 3 × 3: the multipliers go straight into L

For the three-fund portfolio, Lesson 2 gave $E_{32}E_{31}E_{21}A = U$. Undo the three steps in reverse order (topic 1):

$$ A = E_{21}^{-1}\,E_{31}^{-1}\,E_{32}^{-1}\,U = LU. $$

Each inverse just flips the sign of its multiplier, and the product comes out remarkably clean:

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 12 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{E_{21}^{-1}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{E_{31}^{-1}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{E_{32}^{-1}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \mathbf{12} & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf{1} & \mathbf{0.2} & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{L} $$ $$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 & 15 \\ 1 & 0 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 12 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0.2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{L}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & -0.4 \end{bmatrix}}_{U} $$

Check row 3: $1 \times (1, 1, 1) + 0.2 \times (0, -5, 3) + 1 \times (0, 0, -0.4) = (1, 1 - 1, 1 + 0.6 - 0.4) = (1, 0, 1.2)$ ✓, the exposure rule.

The key fact of this lesson

With no row exchanges, the multipliers go directly into $L$: $\ell_{21} = 12$, $\ell_{31} = 1$, $\ell_{32} = 0.2$ sit in exactly their positions. To get $L$ there is no work at all, just keep a record of the multipliers as you eliminate.

Why L is clean but E is messy

Compare with the single elimination matrix from Lesson 2:

$$ E = E_{32}E_{31}E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -12 & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf{1.4} & -0.2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\text{vs}\qquad L = E^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 12 & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf{1} & 0.2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Where does $E$'s $1.4$ come from? Going forward, row 3 had 1 × row 1 removed directly, and then lost 0.2 × the new row 2, which itself was "row 2 − 12 × row 1". That second step quietly added back $0.2 \times 12 = 2.4$ of row 1. Net effect on row 1: $-1 + 2.4 = +1.4$. The steps interfere.

Going backward with $L$, nothing interferes: each original row is simply "its multipliers × the rows of $U$ above it, plus its own row of $U$". Strang: "the multipliers just sit in the matrix $L$." This is why software stores $L$ and $U$, never $E$.

Check yourself (MIT problem 4.1): find $L$ and $U$ for $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 3 & 0 \\ 2 & 4 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$.

Multipliers: $\ell_{21} = 2$ gives row 2 $= (0, -2, 0)$; $\ell_{31} = 2$ gives row 3 $= (0, -6, 1)$; $\ell_{32} = -6/-2 = 3$ gives row 3 $= (0, 0, 1)$. So $U = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and $L = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$, the multipliers 2, 2, 3 in place.

Method · Gauss–Jordan elimination (preview)

Eliminate above each pivot as well as below, turning $[A \mid I]$ into $[I \mid A^{-1}]$. MIT's solution to problem 4.1 finds $E^{-1} = L$ this way. We don't need it here, because $L$ can be read straight off the multipliers. Full treatment in Lesson 3.

4. Pulling out the pivots: A = LDU

Method · LDU decomposition

$A = LDU$: unit lower triangular $L$, diagonal $D$ holding the pivots, unit upper triangular $U$. The same information as $LU$, arranged symmetrically.

$L$ has 1s on its diagonal but $U$ has the pivots. To balance them, divide each row of $U$ by its pivot and collect the pivots in a diagonal matrix $D$:

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & -0.4 \end{bmatrix}}_{U} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -0.4 \end{bmatrix}}_{D}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -0.6 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{new } U} $$

Row 2 of $U$ divided by $-5$ is $(0, 1, -0.6)$; row 3 divided by $-0.4$ is $(0, 0, 1)$. Now $A = LDU$, with 1s on the diagonals of both triangular factors and the pivots $1, -5, -0.4$ in the middle. Strang mostly stays with $A = LU$; $LDU$ is the same information arranged symmetrically.

5. Solving with L and U

Method · Forward and back substitution

Forward substitution solves the lower triangular $Lc = b$ from the top row down. Back substitution then solves $Ux = c$ from the bottom row up. Together they solve $Ax = b$ in about $n^2$ steps once $A = LU$ is known.

Once $A = LU$ is known, solving $Ax = b$ means solving $L(Ux) = b$. Call $Ux = c$ and do it in two easy triangular passes:

  1. Forward: solve $Lc = b$ from the top down. (This is exactly what elimination did to $b$.)
  2. Back: solve $Ux = c$ from the bottom up. (Back substitution, as in Lesson 2.)

Client 1: ₹10 lakh, ₹1,11,000 return, 7.4 exposure

$b = (10, 111, 7.4)$. Forward, $Lc = b$:

  • $c_1 = 10$.
  • $12c_1 + c_2 = 111$, so $c_2 = 111 - 120 = -9$.
  • $1c_1 + 0.2c_2 + c_3 = 7.4$, so $c_3 = 7.4 - 10 + 1.8 = -0.8$.

Back, $Ux = c$: $-0.4z = -0.8$ gives $z = 2$; $-5y + 3(2) = -9$ gives $y = 3$; $x + 3 + 2 = 10$ gives $x = 5$. ₹5 lakh Nifty, ₹3 lakh bonds, ₹2 lakh midcap, as before. Notice $c = (10, -9, -0.8)$ is exactly the right-hand side elimination produced in Lesson 2.

Client 2: same funds, a new target

$b = (10, 98, 5.2)$: ₹10 lakh, ₹98 thousand return, 5.2 exposure. No new elimination, just the two passes with the same $L$ and $U$:

  • Forward: $c_1 = 10$; $c_2 = 98 - 12(10) = -22$; $c_3 = 5.2 - 10 - 0.2(-22) = 5.2 - 10 + 4.4 = -0.4$.
  • Back: $-0.4z = -0.4$ gives $z = 1$; $-5y + 3(1) = -22$ gives $y = 5$; $x + 5 + 1 = 10$ gives $x = 4$.

₹4 lakh Nifty, ₹5 lakh bonds, ₹1 lakh midcap, the answer Lesson 1 found for this target. Check: $48 + 35 + 15 = 98$ ✓.

Client 3: ₹15 lakh

$b = (15, 159, 9.6)$. Forward: $c = (15,\ 159 - 180,\ 9.6 - 15 + 4.2) = (15, -21, -1.2)$. Back: $z = 3$, then $-5y + 9 = -21$ so $y = 6$, then $x = 15 - 6 - 3 = 6$. ₹6 lakh, ₹6 lakh, ₹3 lakh.

Factor once, solve many

The hard part, elimination on $A$, was done once. Each new client costs only two triangular passes. With many targets and the same funds, $A = LU$ is how it's done in practice.

6. How expensive is elimination?

Rule · Operation count: $O(n^3)$ to factor, $O(n^2)$ to solve

Gaussian elimination / LU on an $n \times n$ matrix takes about $\tfrac13 n^3$ multiply-subtract steps; each right-hand side then costs about $n^2$. Read $O(n^3)$ as "grows like $n^3$".

A practical question Strang asks: for an $n \times n$ matrix, how many operations does elimination take? Count one "multiply a row entry and subtract" as one operation.

  • Clearing column 1 changes almost every entry below row 1: about $n^2$ operations.
  • Column 2 works on a matrix one size smaller: about $(n - 1)^2$. Then $(n - 2)^2$, and so on down to $1^2$.
$$ n^2 + (n-1)^2 + \cdots + 2^2 + 1^2 \;\approx\; \int_0^n x^2\,dx \;=\; \tfrac13 n^3. $$
The cost

Factoring $A = LU$: about $\tfrac13 n^3$ operations. Each right-hand side $b$ (forward + back): about $n^2$. Doubling the size of the problem makes factoring 8 times as expensive, but each new target only 4 times.

Strang's aside: computing with determinants instead would cost about $n!$ operations, astronomically more. Elimination is the method that scales.

7. Row exchanges: PA = LU

Method · PA = LU (LU with row exchanges)

Put the rows in a workable order first with a permutation $P$, then factor: $PA = LU$. Every invertible matrix has one. Software picks $P$ by partial pivoting (largest entry in each pivot position) for accuracy.

So far, no row exchanges. What if a 0 lands in a pivot position? Lesson 2's credit fund (12% return, beta 0) did exactly that. The fix: do the needed row exchange first, then factor. For the credit-fund matrix, swap rows 2 and 3 with $P_{23}$:

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{P_{23}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 12 & 15 \\ 1 & 0 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 12 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{L}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 0.2 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{U} $$

After the swap the rows are budget, exposure, return. The multipliers are $\ell_{21} = 1$ (exposure − budget), $\ell_{31} = 12$ (return − 12 × budget), and $\ell_{32} = 0$ (that row was already done). They sit in $L$ as before.

PA = LU

Every invertible matrix can be factored as $PA = LU$, where $P$ puts the rows in an order that needs no exchanges during elimination.

The family of permutation matrices

For 3 × 3 there are exactly 6 permutation matrices, all the ways to reorder the rows of $I$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0&0&1\\0&1&0\\1&0&0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&0\\0&0&1\\0&1&0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0&1&0\\0&0&1\\1&0&0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0 \end{bmatrix} $$

The identity (no change), three single swaps, and two cycles that move all three rows. For $n \times n$ there are $n!$ of them: 24 for 4 × 4.

  • Closed family: multiply two permutations and you get another one (reordering twice is still a reordering). Inverses stay in the family too. Mathematicians call this a group.
  • Inverse = transpose: $P^{-1} = P^T$. A single swap is its own inverse; the two cycles are each other's inverses.
Check yourself: why is $P^{-1} = P^T$ for $P = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0&1&0\\0&0&1\\1&0&0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$?

$P$ sends row 2 to the top, row 3 to the middle, row 1 to the bottom. $P^T = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ sends them back: $P^TP = I$.

8. Singular matrices and LU

A point from the recitation: a singular matrix can still have an LU factorization. What fails is not the factoring but the solving. Take Lesson 2's balanced fund (half Nifty, half bonds):

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 & 9.5 \\ 1 & 0 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 12 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0.2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{L}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 & -2.5 \\ 0 & 0 & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix}}_{U} $$

Elimination never needed a row exchange, so $L$ and $U$ exist, and $L$ is identical to the good portfolio's (the first two columns are the same funds). The difference shows up at the bottom of $U$: a 0 where the third pivot should be. Forward substitution works ($c = (10, -9, -0.8)$), but back substitution hits $0 \cdot z = -0.8$: no solution.

Watch out

"Has an LU factorization" does not mean "invertible". Look at the diagonal of $U$: all pivots non-zero ⇔ invertible.

9. Summary

Key ideas

  1. $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ and $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$: reverse order. (topic 1)
  2. Elimination $EA = U$ rewritten as $A = LU$ with $L = E^{-1}$: lower triangular, 1s on the diagonal. (topic 2)
  3. With no row exchanges, the multipliers go straight into $L$. For the portfolio: 12, 1, 0.2. $E$ is messy (1.4), $L$ is clean. (topic 3)
  4. $A = LDU$ splits the pivots into a diagonal $D$. (topic 4)
  5. Solve $Ax = b$ as $Lc = b$ (forward) then $Ux = c$ (back). New targets reuse $L$ and $U$. (topic 5)
  6. Factoring costs about $n^3/3$ operations; each new right-hand side about $n^2$. (topic 6)
  7. Row exchanges: $PA = LU$. There are $n!$ permutation matrices, and $P^{-1} = P^T$. (topic 7)
  8. A singular matrix can have $A = LU$; the zero pivot appears on $U$'s diagonal. (topic 8)

The other two examples from Lesson 1, now factored. The theme of both: the matrix stays the same (same hedges, same products) while the target changes, which is exactly where $A = LU$ pays off.

Example 1: Hedging, rebalanced every day

Your hedges are fixed: Nifty futures, Nifty IT futures and Bank Nifty futures, with risk profiles $(1, 0, 0)$, $(1, 1, 0)$ and $(1, 0, 1)$ for (market, currency, rates). Your portfolio's exposures change every day.

Factor once

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{L = I}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{U = A} $$

Every multiplier is 0 (nothing below the diagonal to clear), so $L = I$ and $U = A$. Forward substitution does nothing ($c = b$); only back substitution is left.

Solve every day

DayExposures $b$ (market, currency, rates)Back substitutionSell (Nifty, IT, Bank Nifty)
Monday$(10, 3, 2)$$z = 2$, $y = 3$, $x = 10 - 3 - 2 = 5$$(5, 3, 2)$
Tuesday$(12, 4, 1)$$z = 1$, $y = 4$, $x = 12 - 4 - 1 = 7$$(7, 4, 1)$
Wednesday$(9, 2, 3)$$z = 3$, $y = 2$, $x = 9 - 2 - 3 = 4$$(4, 2, 3)$

Three days, three hedges, one factorization. A real desk with hundreds of risk factors works the same way: factor the hedge matrix once, then re-solve each time the exposures move.

If the rules are listed in another order: PA = LU

List the currency rule first and the matrix starts with a 0 pivot. The fix from Lesson 2 becomes part of the factorization:

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{P_{12}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} = \underbrace{I}_{L}\;\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{U} $$

Each day, reorder $b$ the same way ($Pb$), then solve as above.

Check yourself: Thursday's exposures are $(15, 5, 5)$. What do you sell?

$z = 5$, $y = 5$, $x = 15 - 5 - 5 = 5$: sell 5 of each.

Example 2: Lot sizes, many client payoffs

Your products are fixed: futures, the 19,900 call and the 20,100 call. Each client asks for a different payoff across the three expiry levels 19,800 / 20,000 / 20,200.

Factor once

Lesson 2's elimination used $\ell_{21} = 0$, $\ell_{31} = -1$, $\ell_{32} = 3$. They go straight into $L$:

$$ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} -200 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 100 & 0 \\ 200 & 300 & 100 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{L}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} -200 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 100 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 100 \end{bmatrix}}_{U} $$

Check row 3: $-1 \times (-200, 0, 0) + 3 \times (0, 100, 0) + (0, 0, 100) = (200, 300, 100)$ ✓.

Solve for each client

For each target $b$: forward $c_1 = b_1$, $c_2 = b_2$, $c_3 = b_3 + c_1 - 3c_2$; then back $z = c_3/100$, $y = c_2/100$, $x = c_1/(-200)$.

ClientPayoff wanted $b$Forward: $c$Lots (futures, 19,900 C, 20,100 C)
A$(-200, 100, 700)$$(-200,\ 100,\ 700 - 200 - 300) = (-200, 100, 200)$$(1, 1, 2)$
B$(0, 100, 500)$$(0,\ 100,\ 500 + 0 - 300) = (0, 100, 200)$$(0, 1, 2)$
C$(-400, 200, 900)$$(-400,\ 200,\ 900 - 400 - 600) = (-400, 200, -100)$$(2, 2, -1)$

Client C's answer has $-1$: sell (write) one lot of the 20,100 call. Check: $2(-200, 0, 200) + 2(0, 100, 300) - 1(0, 0, 100) = (-400, 200, 900)$ ✓.

Singular: futures, call and put at one strike

The parity matrix needs a row exchange and then still runs out of pivots. Factoring still works:

$$ P_{23}A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -200 & 0 & 200 \\ 0 & 200 & 200 \\ 0 & 0 & \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix} $$

But the 0 on $U$'s diagonal means no client payoff that is non-zero at 20,000 can ever be built: back substitution gets stuck on the last row.

Check yourself: which lots give the payoff $(-200, 0, 200)$?

Forward: $c = (-200,\ 0,\ 200 - 200 - 0) = (-200, 0, 0)$. Back: $z = 0$, $y = 0$, $x = 1$. Just one lot of futures, whose payoff is exactly $(-200, 0, 200)$.

Pick an example and the lab factors it: $A = LU$ (or $PA = LU$ when a row exchange is needed), then shows the forward and back solves. Change only the target $b$ and watch $L$ and $U$ stay exactly the same.
