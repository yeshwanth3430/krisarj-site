Column Space and Nullspace

Every matrix carries two spaces. The column space answers "which targets can I reach?". The nullspace answers "which trades change nothing?". Together they describe every answer to $Ax = b$.

Part 3 Explanation

The whole idea in one line

Column space: which targets can my funds reach? Nullspace: which trades change nothing at all? Every portfolio that hits a target = one portfolio that hits it + any do-nothing trade.

The running example: the same funds, one new one

Rules (rows): budget (₹ lakh), return (₹ thousand), market exposure. Each fund is a column: what ₹1 lakh of it does to the three rules.

FundReturnBetaColumn (budget, return, exposure)

Bonds7%0$(1, 7, 0)$
Midcap15%1.2$(1, 15, 1.2)$
Balanced (half Nifty, half bonds)9.5%0.5$(1, 9.5, 0.5)$

We'll use two fund matrices: one with the balanced fund (it repeats what Nifty and bonds already do) and one with midcap (it adds something new).

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 & 9.5 \\ 1 & 0 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix} \text{ (Nifty, bonds, balanced)} \qquad A_{\text{mid}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 & 15 \\ 1 & 0 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix} \text{ (Nifty, bonds, midcap)} $$

1. When can Ax = b be solved?

The whole idea in one line

$Ax$ is always a mix of the columns of $A$. So $Ax = b$ has an answer exactly when $b$ is a mix of the columns, that is, when $b$ is in the column space $C(A)$.

Step 1: Ax is a mix of the columns

Hold ₹7 lakh Nifty, ₹3 lakh bonds, nothing in balanced: $x = (7, 3, 0)$. Row by row:

$$ Ax = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 & 9.5 \\ 1 & 0 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1(7) + 1(3) + 1(0) \\ 12(7) + 7(3) + 9.5(0) \\ 1(7) + 0(3) + 0.5(0) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 + 3 + 0 \\ 84 + 21 + 0 \\ 7 + 0 + 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 105 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Column by column, the same product is $7 \times$ (Nifty column) $+\ 3 \times$ (bonds column) $+\ 0 \times$ (balanced column). Whatever $x$ you pick, $Ax$ is some mix of the columns. That's all a matrix can produce.

Step 2: so the target must be a mix too

Rule · Ax = b is solvable exactly when b is in C(A)

If $Ax = b$ has a solution, then $b = Ax$ is a mix of the columns, so $b \in C(A)$. If $b \in C(A)$, the amounts of each column in that mix are a solution $x$. In money terms: a target can be hit exactly when it's a target your funds can reach.

A handy way to build reachable targets: pick any $x$ first, then compute $b = Ax$. Every $b$ you make this way is in $C(A)$, and you already know one answer for it.

Step 3: MIT's example: 4 rules, 3 unknowns

Strang's matrix has four rows (four equations) and only three columns (three unknowns):

$$ A_{\text{MIT}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 4 & 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}. $$

  1. What Its columns live in $\mathbb{R}^4$ (four entries each). Can three columns reach every target in $\mathbb{R}^4$?

    Why not Three directions can fill at most a 3D slice of a 4D space. Some targets must be out of reach.

  2. Look closer Column 3 = column 1 + column 2: $1 + 1 = 2$, $2 + 1 = 3$, $3 + 1 = 4$, $4 + 1 = 5$ ✓.

    So Column 3 adds no new direction. $C(A_{\text{MIT}})$ is only the plane of mixes $a(1, 2, 3, 4) + c(1, 1, 1, 1) = (a + c,\ 2a + c,\ 3a + c,\ 4a + c)$, a 2D plane inside $\mathbb{R}^4$.

  3. Reachable Each entry is $a$ more than the one before, so the four entries must go up in equal steps. $(1, 2, 3, 4)$: steps 1, 1, 1 ✓. $(2, 3, 4, 5)$ ✓.

    Not reachable $(1, 0, 0, 0)$: steps $-1, 0, 0$, not equal ✗. No $x$ solves $A_{\text{MIT}}x = (1, 0, 0, 0)$.

Step 4: the same thing with our funds

The balanced fund is half Nifty, half bonds: $\tfrac12(1, 12, 1) + \tfrac12(1, 7, 0) = (0.5 + 0.5,\ 6 + 3.5,\ 0.5 + 0) = (1, 9.5, 0.5)$ ✓. Like column 3 above, it adds no new direction. So $C(A)$ is still the plane from Lesson 5: targets with $R = 7B + 5E$. The target $(10, 105, 7)$ passes ($70 + 35 = 105$); $(10, 105, 5)$ still fails ($70 + 25 = 95$).

In one line

A target is hittable exactly when it's in $C(A)$; a column that's a mix of the others makes $C(A)$ no bigger.

2. The nullspace: trades that change nothing

The whole idea in one line

The nullspace $N(A)$ is every trade $x$ with $Ax = 0$: trades that move no rule at all. Same budget, same return, same exposure, just different funds.

Method · Nullspace N(A)

For a matrix $A$ with $n$ columns, $N(A)$ is the set of all $x$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $Ax = 0$. It always contains $x = 0$ ("do nothing"). The question is whether it contains anything else.

Step 1: two twin index funds (2 × 2)

Two Nifty index funds from different fund houses do exactly the same thing. With just the budget and return rules, both columns are $(1, 12)$:

$$ A_{\text{twin}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 12 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{solve } A_{\text{twin}}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. $$
Method · Elimination on Ax = 0

Eliminate as usual (the right side is all zeros and stays zeros, so we leave it out). A column with no pivot gives a free unknown: choose it, then back-substitute for the rest. No free unknown means the only answer is $x = 0$.

  1. What we do Clear $x$ out of row 2.

    How Multiplier $= 12 / 1 = 12$. Row 2 $-\ 12 \times$ row 1: $12 - 12(1) = 0$, $12 - 12(1) = 0$.

    Now Row 2 is $0x + 0y = 0$: always true, no information. Column 2 has no pivot, so $y$ is free.

  2. Choose $y = -1$. Row 1: $x + (-1) = 0$, so $x = 1$.

    Check Budget: $1(1) + 1(-1) = 0$ ✓. Return: $12(1) + 12(-1) = 0$ ✓.

    Meaning $N(A_{\text{twin}})$ = all multiples $c(1, -1)$: move ₹$c$ lakh from one twin to the other. Nothing changes. A line of do-nothing trades.

Compare Nifty and bonds, $\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 12 & 7 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$: row 2 $-\ 12 \times$ row 1 gives $12 - 12 = 0$ and $7 - 12 = -5$, so row 2 reads $-5y = 0$. Then $y = 0$ and row 1 gives $x = 0$. Only the zero trade: $N = \{0\}$. Different funds, so nothing can be swapped for free.

Step 2: Nifty, bonds and balanced (3 × 3)

Solve $Ax = 0$ for $A$ = (Nifty, bonds, balanced), one entry at a time.

  1. What we do Clear Nifty out of the return row (row 2).

    Why The pivot is the 1 in row 1, column 1.

    How Multiplier $= 12 / 1 = 12$. Row 2 $-\ 12 \times$ row 1: $12 - 12(1) = 0$, $\;7 - 12(1) = -5$, $\;9.5 - 12(1) = -2.5$.

    Now Row 2 $= (0, -5, -2.5)$.

  2. What we do Clear Nifty out of the exposure row (row 3).

    How Multiplier $= 1 / 1 = 1$. Row 3 $-\ 1 \times$ row 1: $1 - 1 = 0$, $\;0 - 1 = -1$, $\;0.5 - 1 = -0.5$.

    Now Row 3 $= (0, -1, -0.5)$.

  3. What we do Clear bonds out of row 3, using the pivot $-5$ in row 2.

    How Multiplier $= (-1) / (-5) = 0.2$. Row 3 $-\ 0.2 \times$ row 2: $0 - 0.2(0) = 0$, $\;-1 - 0.2(-5) = -1 + 1 = 0$, $\;-0.5 - 0.2(-2.5) = -0.5 + 0.5 = 0$.

    Now Row 3 is all zeros. Column 3 (balanced) has no pivot, so the balanced amount $z$ is free.

$$ U = \begin{bmatrix} \boxed{1} & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & \boxed{-5} & -2.5 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \text{pivots in columns 1 and 2; column 3 is free.} $$

  1. Choose $z = -2$ (any number works; $-2$ keeps the others whole).

    Row 2 $-5y - 2.5(-2) = 0 \Rightarrow -5y + 5 = 0 \Rightarrow y = 1$.

    Row 1 $x + 1 + (-2) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 1$.

  2. Check Budget: $1(1) + 1(1) + 1(-2) = 1 + 1 - 2 = 0$ ✓. Return: $12(1) + 7(1) + 9.5(-2) = 12 + 7 - 19 = 0$ ✓. Exposure: $1(1) + 0(1) + 0.5(-2) = 1 + 0 - 1 = 0$ ✓.

    Meaning Buy ₹1 lakh Nifty and ₹1 lakh bonds, sell ₹2 lakh balanced. You have just built the balanced fund yourself, so nothing changes.

Every multiple works too: $c(1, 1, -2)$. So $N(A)$ is a line through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^3$, the space of trades. You'll see it drawn in topic 5.

In one line

The nullspace collects the do-nothing trades; a free column in elimination is a sign it holds more than zero.

3. Why the nullspace is a subspace

Run the subspace test from Lesson 5 on "all trades that change nothing".

Rule · N(A) is always a subspace

If $Av = 0$ and $Aw = 0$, then $A(v + w) = Av + Aw = 0 + 0 = 0$ and $A(cv) = c\,Av = c \cdot 0 = 0$. And $A0 = 0$. So sums, multiples and zero all stay inside.

  1. Zero Doing nothing changes nothing: $A0 = 0$ ✓.

    Sums Do one do-nothing trade, then another: still nothing changed. $(1, 1, -2) + (2, 2, -4) = (3, 3, -6)$, and $A(3, 3, -6) = (3 + 3 - 6,\ 36 + 21 - 57,\ 3 + 0 - 3) = (0, 0, 0)$ ✓.

    Multiples Do it 5 times bigger, or in reverse: $-5 \times (1, 1, -2) = (-5, -5, 10)$, still $0$ ✓.

Notice this works for every matrix, without solving anything. That's why Strang calls the nullspace and the column space the two subspaces every matrix comes with. They live in different places:

Column space $C(A)$Nullspace $N(A)$
Made oftargets $b$ (outputs)trades $x$ (inputs)
Lives in$\mathbb{R}^m$: one entry per rule (row)$\mathbb{R}^n$: one entry per fund (column)
QuestionWhich targets can I reach?Which trades change nothing?
How you get itAll mixes of the columnsAll solutions of $Ax = 0$
Nifty, bonds, balancedthe plane $R = 7B + 5E$the line through $(1, 1, -2)$

For MIT's $4 \times 3$ matrix the two spaces aren't even the same size of vector: $C(A_{\text{MIT}})$ sits in $\mathbb{R}^4$, and $N(A_{\text{MIT}})$ sits in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Its nullspace is the line through $(1, 1, -1)$ (column 1 + column 2 − column 3 $= 0$): row by row, $1 + 1 - 2 = 0$, $2 + 1 - 3 = 0$, $3 + 1 - 4 = 0$, $4 + 1 - 5 = 0$ ✓.

In one line

Do-nothing trades add and scale into do-nothing trades, so $N(A)$ is always a subspace, of the trade space $\mathbb{R}^n$.

4. When the nullspace is only zero

Swap the balanced fund for midcap. Does anything still change nothing?

  1. Row 2 Multiplier $12$. Row 2 $-\ 12 \times$ row 1: $12 - 12 = 0$, $\;7 - 12 = -5$, $\;15 - 12 = 3$. Now $(0, -5, 3)$.

    Row 3 Multiplier $1$. Row 3 $-$ row 1: $1 - 1 = 0$, $\;0 - 1 = -1$, $\;1.2 - 1 = 0.2$. Now $(0, -1, 0.2)$.

  2. Row 3 again Multiplier $(-1)/(-5) = 0.2$. Row 3 $-\ 0.2 \times$ row 2: $0$, $\;-1 - 0.2(-5) = 0$, $\;0.2 - 0.2(3) = 0.2 - 0.6 = -0.4$.

    Now $U = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -5 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & -0.4 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$: three pivots, no free column.

  3. Back-substitute Row 3: $-0.4z = 0 \Rightarrow z = 0$. Row 2: $-5y + 3(0) = 0 \Rightarrow y = 0$. Row 1: $x + 0 + 0 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0$.

    Meaning $N(A_{\text{mid}}) = \{0\}$. No trade among these three funds leaves all three rules untouched.

Rule · N(A) = {0} means no column is redundant

A non-zero $x$ in $N(A)$ says "this mix of columns adds to zero", so one column can be rebuilt from the others. $N(A) = \{0\}$ says no column can be rebuilt: each adds a new direction. (Lesson 9 names this independence.)

Why a trader cares. When $N(A) = \{0\}$, each reachable target has exactly one portfolio: if $Ax = b$ and $Ay = b$, then $A(x - y) = 0$, so $x - y = 0$. That's why every client in Lessons 1–4 got a single answer. With the balanced fund, there's never just one answer, as the next topic shows.

For the curious: midcap breaks the link, and a free trade appears

Midcap doesn't obey $R = 7B + 5E$ ($7 + 6 = 13 \ne 15$). Take the trade $(-6, 1, 5)$ from Lesson 5: $A_{\text{mid}}(-6, 1, 5) = (-6 + 1 + 5,\ -72 + 7 + 75,\ -6 + 0 + 6) = (0, 10, 0)$. It changes only the return. That's a direction the balanced fund could never give, and it's why $C(A_{\text{mid}})$ is all of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

In one line

No free column ⇒ $N(A) = \{0\}$ ⇒ every reachable target has one, and only one, portfolio.

5. All solutions: one answer + the nullspace

The whole idea in one line

Find one portfolio that hits the target. Add any do-nothing trade and it still hits it. That gives all of them.

Step 1: one answer

Target $b = (10, 105, 7)$ with Nifty, bonds, balanced. From topic 1 we already know one answer: $x_p = (7, 3, 0)$ (the "p" is for particular).

Step 2: add the do-nothing trade

Rule · All solutions = particular + nullspace

If $Ax_p = b$ and $Ax_n = 0$, then $A(x_p + x_n) = b + 0 = b$. And every solution comes this way: if $Ax = b$, then $A(x - x_p) = b - b = 0$, so $x - x_p$ is in $N(A)$. Here: $x = (7, 3, 0) + t(1, 1, -2) = (7 + t,\ 3 + t,\ -2t)$.

$t$Portfolio (Nifty, bonds, balanced)BudgetReturnExposure
0$(7, 3, 0)$$7 + 3 + 0 = 10$$84 + 21 + 0 = 105$$7 + 0 + 0 = 7$
1$(8, 4, -2)$$8 + 4 - 2 = 10$$96 + 28 - 19 = 105$$8 + 0 - 1 = 7$
$-1$$(6, 2, 2)$$6 + 2 + 2 = 10$$72 + 14 + 19 = 105$$6 + 0 + 1 = 7$
$-3$$(4, 0, 6)$$4 + 0 + 6 = 10$$48 + 0 + 57 = 105$$4 + 0 + 3 = 7$

Four different portfolios, all with ₹10 lakh, ₹105 thousand and exposure 7. There are infinitely many: one for every $t$.

Try it: slide along the solutions

Step 3: these solutions are not a subspace

  1. Zero test Is $(0, 0, 0)$ a solution? It would need $7 + t = 0$ and $3 + t = 0$ at once: $t = -7$ and $t = -3$. Impossible ✗. (Also, doing nothing has budget 0, not 10.)

    Sum test $(7, 3, 0) + (6, 2, 2) = (13, 5, 2)$. Budget $13 + 5 + 2 = 20$, return $156 + 35 + 19 = 210$, exposure $13 + 0 + 1 = 14$: that's $2b$, not $b$ ✗.

    Picture The green line is the purple line shifted off the origin. A shifted line misses $0$, like the fixed-budget line in Lesson 5.

MIT's version: $A_{\text{MIT}}x = (1, 2, 3, 4)$ is solved by $(1, 0, 0)$, and by $(1, 0, 0) + c(1, 1, -1)$ for every $c$. At $c = -1$ that's $(0, -1, 1)$: $-1(1, 1, 1, 1) + 1(2, 3, 4, 5) = (1, 2, 3, 4)$ ✓. A line through $(1, 0, 0)$ and $(0, -1, 1)$, but not through $0$.

For the curious: which of these portfolios are long-only?

Long-only needs every amount $\ge 0$: $7 + t \ge 0$, $3 + t \ge 0$ and $-2t \ge 0$. The last says $t \le 0$; the second says $t \ge -3$. So only $-3 \le t \le 0$ works: from $(7, 3, 0)$ (no balanced) to $(4, 0, 6)$ (no bonds). The algebra gives a whole line; the "no shorting" rule cuts it to a segment. That cut is what portfolio optimisers work with.

In one line

All answers = one answer + the nullspace: a copy of $N(A)$ shifted to pass through $x_p$. A unique answer happens exactly when $N(A) = \{0\}$.

6. Spotting subspaces: rules that equal zero

The whole idea in one line

Any set described by linear rules that equal zero is a nullspace, so it is automatically a subspace. Change one zero to anything else and it stops being one.

Step 1: zero-cost trades are a nullspace

With Nifty, bonds, midcap, a zero-cost trade has $x + y + z = 0$. That is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = 0$: the nullspace of the $1 \times 3$ matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. One rule, three unknowns, two free: a plane through $0$.

Step 2: market-neutral trades are a nullspace

A trade that adds no market exposure has $1x + 0y + 1.2z = 0$: the nullspace of $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix}$. Another plane through $0$.

Step 3: both at once: stack the rules

Rule · Stacking rules intersects nullspaces (MIT 6.3)

If $C = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} A \\ B \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ (the rows of $A$ on top of the rows of $B$), then $Cx = 0$ means $Ax = 0$ and $Bx = 0$. So $N(C) = N(A) \cap N(B)$, and the intersection of two subspaces is a subspace.

$$ C = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix} \;\begin{matrix} \leftarrow \text{zero cost} \\ \leftarrow \text{zero exposure} \end{matrix} $$

  1. Eliminate Multiplier $1$. Row 2 $-$ row 1: $1 - 1 = 0$, $\;0 - 1 = -1$, $\;1.2 - 1 = 0.2$. Now $\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 0.2 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$: column 3 is free.

    Choose $z = 5$. Row 2: $-y + 0.2(5) = 0 \Rightarrow y = 1$. Row 1: $x + 1 + 5 = 0 \Rightarrow x = -6$.

  2. Check Cost: $-6 + 1 + 5 = 0$ ✓. Exposure: $-6 + 0 + 1.2(5) = -6 + 6 = 0$ ✓.

    Meaning $N(C)$ is the line through $(-6, 1, 5)$: the trade from Lesson 5, now found as a nullspace. Two planes through $0$ meet in a line through $0$.

Linear rules set to zero always carve out a subspace (a nullspace); a non-zero right side, an "or", or a product breaks it.

Check yourself (MIT recitation): which of these sets of $(b_1, b_2, b_3)$ are subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^3$? (1) $b_1 + b_2 - b_3 = 0$; (2) $b_3 = b_1 b_2$; (3) $(1, 0, 0) + c_1(1, 0, -1) + c_2(1, 0, 1)$; (4) $(0, 1, 0) + c_1(1, 0, -1) + c_2(1, 0, 1)$.

(1) Yes: it's the nullspace of $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$. (2) No: $(1, 1, 1)$ is in it ($1 = 1 \times 1$), but $(2, 2, 2)$ isn't ($2 \ne 2 \times 2$). (3) Yes: $(1, 0, 0) = \tfrac12(1, 0, -1) + \tfrac12(1, 0, 1)$ already lies in the plane, so shifting by it changes nothing; the set is just the plane of all $c_1(1, 0, -1) + c_2(1, 0, 1)$. (4) No: every mix of the two vectors has middle entry 0, so adding $(0, 1, 0)$ makes the middle entry 1. The zero vector is never reached.

7. Summary

Key ideas

  1. $Ax$ is always a mix of the columns, so $Ax = b$ is solvable exactly when $b \in C(A)$. (topic 1)
  2. The nullspace $N(A)$ is all $x$ with $Ax = 0$: the trades that change nothing. Find it by elimination: free columns give non-zero solutions. (topic 2)
  3. $N(A)$ is always a subspace, of $\mathbb{R}^n$ (trades); $C(A)$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$ (targets). (topic 3)
  4. $N(A) = \{0\}$ means no redundant column, and one portfolio per reachable target. (topic 4)
  5. All solutions of $Ax = b$ = one particular solution + the nullspace: a shifted copy, not a subspace. (topic 5)
  6. Linear rules "$= 0$" give nullspaces; stacking rules intersects them. (topic 6)

Words in this lesson

Column space $C(A)$
All mixes of the columns of $A$: the reachable targets. A subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$.
Nullspace $N(A)$
All $x$ with $Ax = 0$: the do-nothing trades. A subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
Free column / free unknown
A column with no pivot after elimination; its unknown can be chosen freely.
Particular solution $x_p$
Any one solution of $Ax = b$.
Complete solution
$x = x_p + x_n$ with $x_n$ in $N(A)$: every solution.
Intersection $S \cap T$
Vectors in both $S$ and $T$; a subspace whenever $S$ and $T$ are.

The other two running examples through this lesson's question: which trades change nothing, and what does that do to the answers?

Example 1: Hedging, redundant hedges

Rows: (market, currency, rates) exposure. Hedges: Nifty futures $(1, 0, 0)$, Nifty IT futures $(1, 1, 0)$, Bank Nifty futures $(1, 0, 1)$.

2 × 2: Nifty and IT futures, market and currency only

$\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]x = 0$ is already triangular. Row 2: $y = 0$. Row 1: $x + 0 = 0$, so $x = 0$. $N = \{0\}$: each (market, currency) exposure has exactly one hedge.

3 × 3: the three futures

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Already upper triangular with pivots $1, 1, 1$, so no free column. Row 3: $z = 0$. Row 2: $y = 0$. Row 1: $x + 0 + 0 = 0$, so $x = 0$. $N(A) = \{0\}$: every exposure has one hedge, and none of the three futures can be built from the other two.

Swap Bank Nifty for a spread contract

Suppose the desk is offered an "IT minus Nifty" spread, which carries only currency exposure: $(0, 1, 0)$. Columns: Nifty fut, IT fut, spread.

$$ A_s = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \;\begin{matrix} \leftarrow \text{market} \\ \leftarrow \text{currency} \\ \leftarrow \text{rates} \end{matrix} $$

  1. Pivots Columns 1 and 2. Row 3 is all zeros; column 3 (spread) is free.

    Choose spread $z = 1$. Row 2: $y + 1 = 0 \Rightarrow y = -1$. Row 1: $x + (-1) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 1$.

  2. Check Market: $1 - 1 + 0 = 0$ ✓. Currency: $0 - 1 + 1 = 0$ ✓. Rates: $0$ ✓.

    Meaning Buy 1 Nifty fut, sell 1 IT fut, buy 1 spread: zero exposure everywhere. The spread is "IT minus Nifty", so it's redundant. $N(A_s)$ = all $c(1, -1, 1)$.

  3. Target Hedge $(10, 3, 0)$. One answer: $x_p = (7, 3, 0)$, as in Lesson 5.

    All answers $(7 + t,\ 3 - t,\ t)$. At $t = 3$: $(10, 0, 3)$. Market $10 + 0 + 0 = 10$, currency $0 + 0 + 3 = 3$, rates $0$ ✓. The desk can skip IT futures entirely and use the spread.

    But Rate exposure is still unreachable: every column has 0 in the rates row. Redundancy gives more ways to hit the same targets, not more targets.

Example 2: Lot sizes, put–call parity is a nullspace

Rows: Nifty ends at 19,800 / 20,000 / 20,200. Entries: payoff in points per lot. Columns: futures $(-200, 0, 200)$, 20,000 call $(0, 0, 200)$, 20,000 put $(200, 0, 0)$.

2 × 2: futures and the call, two scenarios

Using only the 19,800 and 20,200 scenarios: $\left[\begin{smallmatrix} -200 & 0 \\ 200 & 200 \end{smallmatrix}\right]x = 0$. Multiplier $200 / (-200) = -1$, so row 2 $+$ row 1: $200 - 200 = 0$, $200 + 0 = 200$. Row 2: $200y = 0 \Rightarrow y = 0$. Row 1: $-200x = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0$. $N = \{0\}$.

3 × 3: futures, call and put

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} -200 & 0 & 200 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 200 & 200 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \;\begin{matrix} \leftarrow 19{,}800 \\ \leftarrow 20{,}000 \\ \leftarrow 20{,}200 \end{matrix} $$

  1. Row 3 Multiplier $200 / (-200) = -1$, so row 3 $+$ row 1: $200 - 200 = 0$, $\;200 + 0 = 200$, $\;0 + 200 = 200$. Now $(0, 200, 200)$.

    Swap Row 2 is all zeros (nothing pays at 20,000). Swap rows 2 and 3 to bring the pivot up: $U = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} -200 & 0 & 200 \\ 0 & 200 & 200 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Column 3 (put) is free.

  2. Choose put $z = 1$. Row 2: $200y + 200 = 0 \Rightarrow y = -1$. Row 1: $-200x + 200 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 1$.

    Check 19,800: $-200 - 0 + 200 = 0$ ✓. 20,000: $0$ ✓. 20,200: $200 - 200 + 0 = 0$ ✓.

    Meaning Long 1 future, short 1 call, long 1 put pays nothing anywhere: put–call parity (a conversion). $N(A)$ = all $c(1, -1, 1)$.

Every way to build a payoff

Client payoff $(-200, 0, 600)$. One answer: 1 future + 2 calls, $x_p = (1, 2, 0)$. All answers: $(1 + t,\ 2 - t,\ t)$.

$t$Lots (futures, call, put)at 19,800at 20,000at 20,200
0$(1, 2, 0)$$-200 + 0 + 0 = -200$$0$$200 + 400 + 0 = 600$
1$(2, 1, 1)$$-400 + 0 + 200 = -200$$0$$400 + 200 + 0 = 600$
2$(3, 0, 2)$$-600 + 0 + 400 = -200$$0$$600 + 0 + 0 = 600$

"1 future + 2 calls" and "3 futures + 2 puts" pay the same in every scenario. In real markets the cheaper route wins, and if one route is cheaper than another by more than costs, a desk sells the expensive one and buys the cheap one. That gap is what parity arbitrage trades.
